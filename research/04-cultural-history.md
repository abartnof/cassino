# 04 — Cultural & social history of Cassino (non-rulebook sources)

Scope: how Cassino/Casino shows up outside rulebooks, in literature, letters, diaries, newspapers, slang, film and TV, and in national traditions. Special focus: **table talk** (calls, announcements, disputes) and **figurative/idiomatic uses**.

Conventions:
- Every bullet has a citation `[Sn]` to a source fetched in this session (see "Sources" at the end).
- "FTS snippet" means the passage was read in the archive.org full-text-search result for that item, not in the full book. This happened when the item is lending-restricted.
- OCR quotes from historic newspapers are lightly normalised (hyphenation and obvious OCR noise removed). Words in [brackets] are mine.
- Non-English quotes give the original first, then my English translation.
- Publication years of well-known novels (e.g., *Delta Wedding* 1946, *Tish* 1916) come from general bibliographic knowledge. They were not separately verified. The passages themselves were.

## 1. Origin & naming debate

### 1.1 Earliest records (England, 1790s)
- Wikipedia puts the first record of the game "as 'Cassino'" in **1792 in England**, citing Robert Long's *Short Rules for Playing the Game of Cassino* (London: J. Owens, 1792). It says the game "appears to have become something of a fashionable craze" [S1].
- The 1792 Long booklet is on archive.org as "Short rules for playing the game of cassino; by Robert Long. 1792" [S64].
- Franco Pratesi ("Casino from Nowhere, to Vaguely Everywhere", dated 09.10.1994; printed in *The Playing-Card* XXIV/1, 1995) lists three early treatises [S7]:
  - Long, 1792.
  - *A Mock-Heroic Poem, dedicated by permission to Her Grace the Duchess of Bolton … containing the Laws of the Game of Casino* (1792; enlarged 1793).
  - *Rules for Cassino. Humbly Inscribed to Her Grace the Duchess of Marlborough, by whose Indulgence, and frequently at Her Grace's own Cost, the Inventor was permitted to make repeated trials of his System at Her Grace's Card-table at Marlborough House, Sion and Brightelmstone* (1795).

  His conclusion: "a craze for the game occurred in London in the early years of the 1790s" [S7].
- Pratesi reports the OED's literary citations (from OED 2nd ed., 1989; I have not seen the OED itself) [S7]:
  - "Roberts (1792) mentioning a party based on Pharo and Cassino".
  - "J. Austen (1800) quoting tables of Whist and Cassino".
  - "Lady Middleton (1811) who proposed 'a rubber of Cassino'".
  - "E. Neres (1811)".
  - [The 1800 and 1811 Austen passages are verified below, §2.1. "Roberts 1792" and "Neres 1811" are UNVERIFIED — not found in fetched sources.]
- Etymonline: casino first means (1744) "public room for music or dancing," from Italian *casino*, "a little house." It adds: "The card game (also cassino) is attested by that name from 1792. Specifically as 'building for aristocratic gambling' by 1820, first in an Italian context." It also quotes Jane Waldie Watts (1820): "the term Casino [is] indiscriminately applied to a set of farm offices, a country-seat, a gambling house, and a game of cards" [S8].
- Pratesi: Cassino was in the British *Hoyle* by 1796 ("Hoyle's Games Improved", ed. Charles Jones) and absent from the 1790 edition [S7].
- Parlett's earlier view, in *The Penguin Book of Card Games* (1979 and 1987 printings), was that "Their early history is obscure, but Cassino first appears in an edition of Hoyle in 1808" [S50, FTS snippet]. This date was later pushed back to 1792 (Wikipedia cites Parlett 2008 together with Long 1792) [S1].
- Spread to Central Europe: Wikipedia says the game was in Vienna and Prague rules by 1795, which "incorporated English terminology such as 'sweep' and 'lurch'" [S1]. Pratesi cites *Gesetzbuch der modernen Spiele Casino, Whist, Boston, …* (Wien und Prag, 1795). He reads its title order as "indicative of an actual fashion for the game" [S7].
- Pratesi quotes an 1854 German claim, "Die Benennung dieses Spieles führt von einem Spielhause in Florenz her, worin der Adel sich versammelte" ("the name of this game derives from a gaming-house in Florence, where the nobles gathered") [S7].
- The same Florentine story appears already in the 1793 *Casino; a mock-heroic poem*. Its footnote derives the title from "D'Alberti's Dictionary … 'Espece d'Academie de jeu de la Noblesse a Florence' … Sort of Gaming-House for the Use of the [nobility]" [S21].
- To test this, Pratesi searched the records of the Florentine *Casino de' Nobili* (founded 1640) in the Archivio di Stato. "My research has not been successful, since the games mentioned are known ones, as Piquet, Minchiate, Trentuno, and Whist" [S7].

### 1.2 The "Italian origin" claim
- Pratesi was surprised to find that "this use of the word 'casino' as the name of a specific card game has left no traces in Italy, neither in old handbooks of games, nor in dictionaries." He suggests a commonly played Italian fishing game was "first introduced in England and later modified in America." He ends: "I would be much happier if I could add an exact date, as for instance Florence 1790, but I cannot — yet" [S7].
- Wikipedia: Cassino is "often said, without substantiation, to be of Italian origin." Scopa and Scopone are documented later than Cassino [S1].
- Pagat (John McLeod): "Although it is traditionally supposed to have originated in Italy, there is no direct evidence of it having been played there, at least under that name" [S2].
- Japanese Wikipedia says the same: Cassino resembles Italian Scopa and is thought to have come from Italy, "but there is no positive evidence for this." The oldest literature is late-18th-century London [S90].
- National Wikipedias repeat different origin myths. These are useful as evidence of folk belief, not as facts:
  - Norwegian: "Spillet stammer fra Italia, og er sannsynligvis en avart av … Scopa" ("The game comes from Italy and is probably a variant of … Scopa") [S86].
  - Hungarian: its origin was "egy középkori olasz kártyajáték, a scopa" ("a medieval Italian card game, scopa") [S89].
  - Swedish: "Spelet är av kinesiskt ursprung" ("The game is of Chinese origin") [S85]. Compare Parlett, who says the Fishing family "may be of Chinese origin" [S51].
- Morehead & Mott-Smith also gave a French origin. As quoted by Pratesi, *Hoyle up-to-date* says "Casino descends from French gambling games of the 15th century." Later Culbertson/Goren editions changed this: "One of the first references to it in English is found in Hoyle's Games … 1808. But several very similar games are described in earlier French and German manuals, as Papillon and Callabra" [S7].
- Deeper roots: Parlett calls *Laugh and Lie Down* (Willughby, c.1665) "the earliest known example of a European game of the Fishing family (Cassino, Scopa, etc), which may be of Chinese origin." Its name comes from the rule that a player who can no longer capture must "lay down" the hand, "whereupon the other players are supposed to laugh at you" [S51].

### 1.3 "Casino" as a house, not a gambling hall
- Wikipedia: "an origin in gambling dens appears unlikely since a *casino* in the late 18th century was a summer house or country villa; the name was not transferred to gambling establishments until later" [S1].
- Primary evidence, Lady Anna Miller, *Letters from Italy* (Venice, 1771; publ. 1777) [S37]:
  - "The Cassinos … are small houses of one or two rooms on a floor; neatly fitted up, but never fine."
  - "It is the fashion here for every person of distinction to have one Cassino at least."
  - "Here small Coteries meet, play at cards, generally sup together."
  - She writes of "these Cassino parties", i.e. Italian card parties held in *casini*, before any record of a card game called Cassino.
- Wiktionary's "casino" entry gives this obsolete sense ("A small house; a pleasure house…, especially in Italy") with Thraliana (1786) and Boswell (1792) quotations [S9].

### 1.4 Spelling fight: Casino vs. Cassino (1793 onward)
- The fight is already on in 1793. The author of the mock-heroic poem attacks "a recent Publication, by a Person calling himself 'Robert Long'". Long "speaks of Three-handed Casino, (or CASSINO as he erroneously spells it,)" [S21].
- Pagat: "the earliest sources use the spelling Casino, but a tradition has grown up among later writers to spell it with a double 's'" [S2].
- Wikipedia says the opposite about which spelling came first: "The spelling 'Cassino' is used in the earliest rules of 1792" [S1]. Both claims fit the evidence: Long (1792) used *Cassino* and the poem (1792/93) used *Casino*.
- Pratesi: "The reason for writing Cassino is said to be a printing mistake in one of the early descriptions." He notes that Italian *cassino* is a rare word meaning "box-cart" [S7]. Wiktionary agrees: Italian *cassino* = "a type of two-wheeled cart" [S9].
- In Brazilian Portuguese, *cassino* is the normal spelling for a gambling house [S9]. A Portuguese dictionary (1964) defines the card game: "Cassino grande, o dez de ouros. Cassino pequeno, o dois de espadas" ("Big cassino, the ten of diamonds. Little cassino, the two of spades") [S83, FTS snippet].
- Taylor's *Portuguese-English Dictionary* (Stanford, 1958) has "cassino … (a card game) … — grande, big cassino (the 10 of diamonds). — pequeno, little cassino (the 2 of clubs)" [S84, FTS snippet]. [The "clubs" disagrees with every other source and may be an error.]

## 2. Cassino in English literature, letters & diaries (1790s–1830s): the Regency "Cassino table"

### 2.1 Jane Austen (three novels and one letter)
- **Letter to Cassandra, Steventon, Thursday 20 November [1800]**, describing a dinner at Ashe: "There was a whist and a casino table, and six outsiders" [S19]. This is the 1800 Austen citation reported from the OED [S7].
- ***Sense and Sensibility* (1811), ch. 23:** "Lady Middleton proposed a rubber of Casino to the others. No one made any objection but Marianne, who … exclaimed, 'Your Ladyship will have the goodness to excuse *me*—you know I detest cards.'" [S17]
- ***Sense and Sensibility*, ch. 28:** at a London party, "Lady Middleton sat down to Cassino" [S17]. Austen uses both spellings in one novel.
- ***Pride and Prejudice* (1813), ch. 29 (Rosings):** "Lady Catherine, Sir William, and Mr. and Mrs. Collins sat down to quadrille; and as Miss De Bourgh chose to play at cassino, the two girls had the honour of assisting Mrs. Jenkinson to make up her party. Their table was superlatively stupid. Scarcely a syllable was uttered that did not relate to the game, except when Mrs. Jenkinson expressed her fears of Miss De Bourgh's being too hot or too cold" [S16].
  - Design note: Austen's joke is that Cassino *table talk* was nothing but game talk.
- ***The Watsons* (unfinished, c.1804):** at the assembly, "It happened close by Lady Osborne's casino table; Mr Howard, who belonged to it, spoke to his nephew" [S18].
- A modern reading: choosing cassino gave Miss de Bourgh "a duller, more isolated social experience" [S60]. That is a blog interpretation, not evidence.

### 2.2 Stage, poem, and social satire (1790s)
- **Frederick Reynolds, *Cheap Living* (Drury Lane, 1797).** Mrs. Scatter says: "I do long for a game of cassino — that is, in the family way — just for a trifle" [S20].
  - Elsewhere in the play someone's time "is so much better employed in playing cassino" [S20, FTS snippet].
  - Another line: "my wife insists on coming to play cassino with you" [S20, FTS snippet].
  - Together these show Cassino as low-stakes domestic play.
- ***Casino; a mock-heroic poem* (1792; 2nd ed. 1793)** is the richest early cultural document [S21]:
  - The game was mocked by whist players: "All hail, thou new and fashionable Game, / Tho' spurn'd by those who scarcely know thy Name! / Ye Amateurs of Whist, and brainless Wits, / Who cry, 'Casino! well enough for Chits!'" ("chits" = children or young girls).
  - On attention: "Casino's incompatible with Flirting"; "call retentive Mem'ry to your Aid, / Vain is your Skill without that thoughtful Maid."
  - The counting cards get personalities: "The Diamond-ten, the Great Casino nam'd, / Two Points demands, and chief in Rank is fam'd; / The Deuce of Spades, Casino's younger Brother."
  - Advice in verse: "(Aces in Hand are ever a Distress)."
  - A pun from the French Revolution period. Court cards can only pair, so you should play them off first: "Know, that Casino is a Democrate; / And, like Tom Paine, exhorts all Subject Slaves, / 'First Kings and Queens dismiss, and then the Knaves'". The poem ends: "Great George and great Casino live for ever!"
  - Sweeping as image: the "Cards-Plebeian … Are swept in one grand Whirlwind from the Plain".
  - Class: "the Knowledge of the Game is at this Time almost confined to the Circles of Fashion".
  - Authority appealed to in a rules dispute: "the Custom of the first Clubs and the first Circles in London".
  - Early vocabulary: last-capture clearance "is called the Sweep"; "The Lurch is Five"; the author calls himself a would-be "Casino-Lycurgus".
- ***Proceedings and Debates of the Parliament of Pimlico* (1799)**, a satire, complains of "evenings at routs or drums, with whist or cassino" and of "concerts, cassino parties, drums, and visiting tours on the sabbath day" [S39].
- **Alethea Lewis, *Plain Sense* (1796).** A young woman finds a house "as dull as a quaker's meeting: I cannot get a party at cassino, but with old Dowagers, the very sight of whom gives me the vapours." Elsewhere: "you must play at cassino with us" [S38].
- Other novels:
  - Mary Charlton, *The Parisian* (1794): "making her cassino party with such eagerness and alacrity" [S43, FTS snippet].
  - Miss Walsh, *The Officer's Daughter* (1810): "In the first drawing-room were cassino parties, all at deep play"; a man "was intreated to join a cassino party, but declined" [S40].

### 2.3 Real letters & diaries (1790s–1800s)
- **Catherine Francis to her mother, Brighton, 18 Oct 1795:** "we joined the party in the evening, and played Cassino till eleven when we always separate. Mrs. Breton says, she is sure if you was here you would become a perfect Mistress of the Game" [S45].
- **Francis family letters, 1804** [S45]:
  - "I am quite considered as one of the old folks … so they make me play at Cassino with my Uncle & Aunt & Mrs. Strode." (Cassino as the game for "old folks".)
  - Another 1804 letter: "We play'd Cassino nevertheless very much at our leisure … waiting the pleasure of his left hand between every card, I won half a crown … half a crown is an exorbitant Sum to be sure at 3d cassino."
  - Stakes: threepence a game.
- **Thomas Boylston Adams (US legation, Berlin), journal 1798:** "Even[ing] at Count Zinzindorff's — Tea & Cards — played Cassino for the first time with Mad: de Haugwitz." Also "Ev[ening] Cassino" [S44]. So the game reached Prussian court society by 1798.
- **Agnes Witts, *An Edinburgh Diary 1793–1798*:** "in the evening, inlisted Mr Venner into the Cassino party" [S41, FTS snippet].
- **George Canning, letter-journal 1793–95:** "dined - and in the evening read - played cassino - supped - and went to bed"; "I stayed and played cassino with them till it was the old man's [bedtime]" [S47, FTS snippet].
- **Lady Nugent's Journal (Jamaica, 1801–05)** [S46, FTS snippet]:
  - "I played cassino, and did all I could to keep the big wigs [in good humour]"
  - "I danced three dances, and then played cassino till 12"
  - "Played cassino, &c. with the party till nine. Then prayers"
  - "play cassino with them after the visitors go away"
- **Martha & Catherine Wilmot in Russia** [S36]:
  - Troitskoe, 24 April 1804: "I have got a little box of Cassino Markers of tortoiseshell for you; I long for an opportunity of sending them." This is a physical scoring aid; see §8.
  - Journal, Monday 21 [August] 1808: "the Princess [Dashkova] had a Cassino party which divided the Company exactly in two."
- **J. G. Lemaistre, *Travels after the Peace of Amiens* (1806):** "in other apartments there were whist and cassino parties" [S42, FTS snippet].
- **Hester Lynch Piozzi, Bath, 29 Oct 1819** [S23]:
  - She sent a nephew a riddle whose answer is a card-table. One couplet gives a nickname for the ten: "The Ten, at Casino, Decemvir we call, / And Aces, at Commerce, take tribute from all."
  - The riddle's first verse puns on it: "For bold Decemvirs here usurp the sway."
  - Here the Great Cassino (the ten) is called the **"Decemvir"**.

### 2.4 The "cassino table" as a fixture of the Regency drawing room (1806–1840)
Archive.org full-text search for "cassino table" brings up a set of novels and magazines [S49, FTS snippets]:
- Henry Card, *Beauford* (1811): "looking over the cassino table"; "We have need of you two at the cassino table."
- *The Master Passion* (1808): "there was a cassino-table waiting for her".
- Kelly, *The Fatalists* (1821): "cut in at a cassino-table". *Scheming* (n.d.): "lost no time in cutting-in at a cassino-table". "Cutting in" means taking a seat when a player rises.
- *Literary Gazette* (1 Aug 1818): "'The game is up.' Released from the cassino table, I walked round the room".
- *Spirit of the English Magazines* (Dec 1826): "the insipid gravities of the cassino table".
- Catherine Gore, *Pin Money* (1831): "I generally manage to make up my cassino table". The same novel has a young woman who has "never touched cards in my life except in a family game of cassino with mamma and yourself".
- Gore, *The Fair of May Fair* (1832): "Lady Mandeville's cassino-table".
- *The Highland Inn* (1839): "the intricate mazes of a Cassino table".
- Isabella Steward, *The Interdict* (1840): "the snug cassino-set and cutter-in of the little room behind".
- T. S. Surr, *A Winter in London* (1806): luring "that votary of Bacchus to a sober game of cassino" [S49].

### 2.5 Dickens and later British fiction
- **Dickens, *David Copperfield* (1850), ch. 11.** The boy David in the King's Bench prison: "In the evening I used to go back to the prison, and walk up and down the parade with Mr. Micawber; or play casino with Mrs. Micawber, and hear reminiscences of her papa and mama" [S22].
- **Lady Sarah Nicolas, *The Cairn* (1846):** "The game of Cassino is thus played in some parts of Germany: — Great Cassino takes sixteen. Little Cassino — fifteen. Every Ace — fourteen. King — thirteen. Queen — twelve. Knave — eleven" [S1; also FTS snippet S49].
- Pratesi says that by the later 19th century English handbooks treated Cassino as "an old traditional game on the way to dying out. Several authors recall when they played Casino in their youth" [S7].
  - Captain Crawley (1876) admits of the game: "Of this game I, personally, know nothing — Non omnia possumus omnes" [S7].
  - *The Popular Recreator* (1874): "Cassino is a very pleasant and agreeable game, especially for young people" [S7].
- **Thackeray, *Notes of a Journey from Cornhill to Grand Cairo* (1846), ch. XI "A Day and Night in Syria":** "others there were playing at casino—a crowd squatted around the squalling gamblers, and talking and looking on with eager interest" [S34]. [Thackeray may be using "casino" loosely for a local game.]
- **Georgette Heyer, *The Toll-Gate* (1954, Regency pastiche)** [S61, FTS snippets]:
  - "Pike-keeping couldn't be worse than playing cassino with that bird-witted boy."
  - "'Play cassino with Ben!' said the Captain."
  - Shows the game's continuing role as a Regency marker in later fiction.

## 3. American popular culture (1820s–1960s)

### 3.1 Early America & "cassino parties"
- A widely reprinted moral tale ran in many papers from 1827 to 1859. A husband in Vienna "had been invited to a cassino party after the concert, notwithstanding which he remained with his wife" [S100]. Examples: *Winchester Virginia Republican* 16 Feb 1827; *Boon's Lick Times* (Mo.) 21 Nov 1840; *Evening Star* (D.C.) 29 Nov 1858; *Boston Weekly Magazine* 1840 [S49].
- *Spirit of the Times* (NY, 19 Jan 1833): at a fancy ball "he was challenged to a game of Cassino by a lady in a low gipsey hat" [S49, FTS snippet].
- Alexander H. Stephens (Confederate Vice-President) at home "sat in the parlor, playing casino with Robert Hull" (E. R. Richardson, *Little Aleck*, 1932) [S79, FTS snippet].
- Civil War camp life, *Weekly North Iowa Times* (McGregor), 7 Oct 1863: a visitor "might find here and there a squad enjoyed in divers games — a game of cassino, dominoes or muggins is very pleasant" [S101].

### 3.2 The 1870s–1920s heyday: "progressive cassino" parties
- Pratesi: after the mid-19th century, Casino begins its "second life" in America, driven by calling and building [S7].
  - The old English game had become so rare in America that it "was called by a specific name: 'The European game is the favorite with those who play merely for recreation, and is known as Set-Back or Rounce Cassino.'"
  - Foster's *Hoyle* (1897) files Cassino among national games: "The pair is just Cassino-America" [S7].
- Local crazes: *Mineral Point Tribune* (Wis.), 8 May 1878: "Cassino is all the rage in Mineral Point. They play it now where little and big cassino make a clean sweep. It's hard on those who get swept" [S102].
- **Progressive cassino parties** were society-page entertainments. Players moved between tables, as in progressive euchre, and prizes were given:
  - *Capital City Courier* (Lincoln, Neb.), 28 Sep 1889: "The evening was pleasantly passed in progressive casino" [S103].
  - *Sacramento Record-Union*, 3 and 12 Mar 1892: progressive casino parties for "about fifty" guests [S104].
  - *Wheeling Daily Intelligencer*, 24 Nov 1892: the Mercantile Club's "progressive cassino party". Prizes: "silver hairpin box", "an opera bag", and others [S105].
  - *St. Paul Daily Globe*, 19 Feb 1893: "Progressive casino was the leading amusement. The head prizes were won by … while [two others] carried off the foot prizes" [S106].
  - *Louisiana Democrat* (Alexandria), 24 Jul 1895: "The Young Men's Hebrew Club gave a progressive Cassino party at their club rooms last Sunday night" [S107].
  - *San Antonio Daily Light*, 17 Feb 1896; *Savannah Morning News*, 21 Mar 1897; *San Francisco Call*, 9 Jan 1898 (New Year's Eve party, "Miss Jacobs took the first prize"); *San Francisco Call*, 31 May 1903 [S108].
  - *St. Louis Republic*, 13 Mar 1904: "The first honors were carried off by the guest of honor … the consolation, a Mexican souvenir of the Alamo" [S109].
  - *Alaska Citizen* (Fairbanks), 29 Apr 1912: a birthday party; "The trophy of the evening, a pretty handbag" [S110].
  - *Western Kansas World*, 20 Jan 1921: "Progressive Casino and dominoes form the enjoyable pastime for many, both old and young" [S111].
- Small-town Maine, *Somerset Reporter* (Skowhegan), 20 Apr 1905: a surprise party where "The evening was spent playing cassino in the progressive way. David Baker scored the most points" [S156].
- **Afternoon "casino parties" for older women, 1930s–40s:**
  - *Wrangell Sentinel* (Alaska), 10 Jun 1938: for an 82nd birthday, "a four-table dessert casino party, casino being Mrs Berg's favorite card game. Her ability was shown when it was found at the end of the games, that Mrs Berg had made high score." Prizes: "consolation prize" and "cut prize" [S157].
  - Similar parties in the same paper, 1932–39 [S157].
  - *Roanoke Rapids Herald* (N.C.), 8 Mar 1945: a "bridge and casino party" with a "high score casino prize" [S157].
- **The meaning flips (1950s):**
  - *Wrangell Sentinel*, 7 Sep 1951, an Elks notice: "There'll be a casino party at the Elks Club Saturday night … If you don't know what a casino party is just bring your money and a barrel—we'll show you!" [S157]
  - *Key West Citizen* 1957–61: "Monte Carlo Casino party" fundraisers [S157].
  - From here on, "casino party" usually means mock gambling, not the card game.
- Status jokes: "athletes at sea view the suggestion of ring pitching with about as much contempt as old-time poker players accord the idea of playing cassino" (syndicated, *Virginia Enterprise* (Minn.), 24 Aug 1906) [S158].
- Everyday settings:
  - Jurors confined in City Hall (in a report that also mentions Alexander R. Shepherd) passed time with "a game of old sledge, and … another quartette whiled away the time in playing cassino" (*Worcester Daily Press*, 7 Dec 1874) [S158].
  - Steamship saloon passengers "are playing cassino at a dining table" (*Cecil Whig*, 10 Aug 1872) [S158].
- Satire of the craze: *Baltimore County Union*, 4 Apr 1903, a society woman's mock diary. "I had been invited to Mrs. Popinjay's progressive casino. Won a miserable little prize of painted china — and yet Mrs. Popinjay is rich! In the evening was treated very rudely … by the Reverend Brummell because I made a wrong play. And he is the man who preached last Sunday on 'Gambling'!" [S112]
- Elko *Daily Independent* (Nev.), 23 Aug 1899: San Francisco society ladies "are giving invitations for a 'select' cigarette and cassino party. This is an evidence that the 'world do move'" [S113].
- Note: "Cassino/Casino Club" in 19th-century US papers usually means a *social* club, not a game club. Examples: a "calico hop" given by the Cassino Club in Nashville, 1873; the "German Cassino Club hall" in Memphis, 1880 [S114]. This follows the German *Kasino* sense of a club.
- Later shift: by the 1960s–70s, a college "Cassino Party" means a mock gambling night. Examples: Evansville *LinC* yearbooks, "Cassino party unleashes gamblin' desires" (1975) and "Union Board's Cassino Party" (1976); the *Royal Purple* yearbook, 1962, "Gambling equipment was rented for a Cassino party at which everyone was given $2,500 play [money]" [S115, FTS snippets].

### 3.3 Everyone played: snapshots
- **Native Americans in Nevada:**
  - *Morning Appeal* (Carson City), 21 Sep 1886: a group "strolled up to a little circle of Washoe Indians who were hard at work over a game of cassino" at Lake Tahoe. One player won everything [S116].
  - Elko *Weekly Independent*, 10 Jun 1894 (from the *Genoa Courier*): "A syndicate of representative Indians purchased a couple of candles … and played cassino for coin till midnight" [S117].
- **Gamblers & money matches:**
  - *Goodland Republic* (Kan.), 9 Feb 1894: "Leo Gow and a Colorado Springs sporting man are matched to play a game of cassino for $25 a side" [S118].
  - *Guthrie Daily Leader*, 13 Sep 1901, on Richard Canfield, "the King of Gamblers": he "may take part at a quiet game of cassino for a cigar or a bottle, but that is as far as he ever goes" [S119].
  - *Press and Daily Dakotaian*, 31 Dec 1885: a woman on a train beats a hosiery drummer at cassino, winning "eleven pairs" of silk stockings [S120].
- **Violence over the game:**
  - *Evening World* (NY), 18 Oct 1902: "New York Man Fires at Chum from Chicago Because of Quarrel About Cassino in Pittsfield Hotel" [S121].
  - *New-York Tribune*, 6 Jan 1900: a murder "was the result of a dispute … over a game of cassino". The loser refused to let the winner order the drinks [S122].
- **Courtship & home:**
  - *Chariton Courier* (Mo.), 6 Feb 1890: "After supper my girl and I played another game of cassino, and then we took another dance" [S123].
  - Rolf Johnson's Nebraska diary (1876–80): "John proposed to play me a game of cassino to see who should escort Thilda home" [S78, FTS snippet].
  - *Edgefield Advertiser* (S.C.), 9 Oct 1884: a 16-year-old "picked 362 lbs. of cotton. And then played cassino two hours with his sisters before he went to bed" [S124].
- **Politicians & celebrities:**
  - *Wood County Reporter* (Wis.), 21 Jun 1883, on the senator son of Simon Cameron: "The senator's only relaxation is card-playing. He can be kept good-natured … with the game of cassino" [S125].

### 3.4 Perceptions: child's game vs. gambler's game
- Pratesi: Casino "has always been considered as one of the very few games admitted to every environment, family included". It also seems "to have been — one of the games preferred by great gamblers" [S7].
- Morehead's *Modern Hoyle* (1944), as quoted by Pratesi: "In the average home Casino is known as a children's game, but among gamblers it is known as the finest two-handed game of skill, and it is often played for high stakes" [S7].
- Ostrow (1945) and Scarne (1965) call it a "hardy perennial". Scarne: it "was greatest in popularity prior to the advent of Gin Rummy" [S7].
- Parlett (1979): "Cassino itself has long been a popular home game in America and is often recommended for children." On its family: "they are adding-up games. This may be why they are recommended for children and this, in turn, may be why their potential depth has gone unnoticed" [S50, FTS snippets].
- Dixon *Evening Telegraph* (Ill.), 22 Aug 1949: "Ed challenged me to a game of Cassino, which is a great game of memory" [S49, FTS snippet].

## 4. TABLE TALK: what people actually say

### 4.1 Calls and announcements (English)
- **"Building [n]" / "building eights".** Pagat says the builder "must announce the capturing number (saying, for example, 'building 5')" [S2]. Pagat's examples [S2]:
  - "building 8"; "building 9" (when raising a build)
  - "building 10" vs "building 5", for a 5 played on a 5: you must say which, because one is a single build of 10 and the other a multiple build of fives.
  - "building nine" when merging builds
- **"Building eights".** *The Complete Book of Card Games* (1993): a player "places the two on the six, and announces 'building eights'" [S161, FTS snippet].
- **"Building Fives".** Parlett, *Card Games for Two* (1978) and *Teach Yourself Card Games* (1994): you lay a card from hand on its match on the table "(announcing 'Building Fives'), then capture both by pairing on your next turn". "If a player plays a Five upon a Five and announces 'Building Fives', that build can be captured only by a Five" [S50, FTS snippets].
- **Stage comedy: counting out loud and "Clean sweep!"** In the English translation of Georges Feydeau's farce, *The Pregnant Pause, or Love's Labor Lost* (archive.org edition dated 1985) [S159, FTS snippets]:
  - Dealing "four apiece and four face up on the [table]"
  - A character "laying a card on a face-up card): Building fives..."
  - The count, read aloud: "Cards... Spades... Ten of diamonds..." / "Deuce... Aces..."
  - The finish: "Clean sweep! You owe me a hundred francs."
  - On stakes: "But I always play casino for a hundred francs a hand!"
  - A good model of in-game voice lines for the end-of-hand count.
- **Historic build calls: "calling 'seven'" vs. "calling 'sevens'", "fives all", "nines".** The *New York Dispatch* answers-to-correspondents column (1877–1880) quotes readers' disputes word for word. They show that players *named* the build aloud, and that singular vs. plural marked a single build vs. a multiple build:
  - 19 Aug 1877, a partnership game: "A plays a four spot, C puts a three on it, calling 'seven;' B plays six, and D, who also holds a seven, plays ace on B's six, and putting it on his partner (C's) seven, calls 'sevens.'" [S155]
  - 1 Apr 1877: "A puts an ace on the four, and to prevent B from building higher he takes the deuce and tray and places them on the four and ace and calls them **fives all**. Can he do so? Yes" [S155]
  - 8 Apr 1877: "A plays the four from his hand on the five on the board, building nine. He then proceeds to take another four and five from the board, making another nine, and adds to the first build, calling '**nines**'" [S155]
  - 17 Oct 1880: "A takes an ace from his hand and lays it on the eight spot, calling it nines; will any other card but the nine spot take it. No. … Can B put an ace on top of the nine and call it ten? He cannot, as the call is nin[es]" [S155]
  - 22 Feb 1880: "J puts an ace on a nine and calls it ten" [S155]
  - 31 Mar 1878: "A throws little cassino on the four spot, and calls it six. B takes the tray from the table and calls it nine" [S155]
  - Design takeaway: the historic call *is* the rule. Saying "nines" (plural) locks a multiple build that cannot be raised. Saying "ten" (singular) makes a single build that can be.
- **"Last".** "The dealer must announce 'last' when dealing the last cards" [S2]. The reason: whoever captures last takes the leftover table cards [S2].
- **"Sweep" / "clear".** Taking every table card is a sweep, which "Some players call … a clear" [S2]. Wiktionary records *sweep* (card games) as "In the game casino, the act of capturing all face-up cards from the table" [S9].
- **"Cash".** "capturing an ace with another ace is called 'cash' by some players" [S2].
- **"Trailing."** Playing a card without building or capturing "is sometimes called trailing" [S2]. In southern Africa this is called **"drifting"** [S6].
- **"For partner".** In the Dominican partnership game, "When building for partner, a player must always announce 'for partner'" [S5].
- **Declaring out ("I'm out", "I have three points, and am out").** Answers-to-correspondents columns show people *calling out* mid-hand, and the disputes that followed:
  - *New York Dispatch*, 14 Aug 1881: "The one that had 18, said, 'I have three points, and am out;' the other says, 'I have 8, and am out'" [S130].
  - *New York Clipper*, as reprinted in the *Pioche Weekly Record* (Nev.), 10 Mar 1883: "Contention often occurs among players in the game of cassino, as to what points will take them out first … 'You can call out at once on spades if they will put you out. Neither cards nor any other point has precedence'" [S131]. The same item appears in the *Daily Nevada State Journal*, 4 Mar 1883 [S132].
  - The *National Police Gazette* answered dozens of such letters (1885–1906) [S133, FTS snippets]:
    - "A takes up big cassino and calls out; B says he cannot call out"
    - "it is B's play and D calls out; can D call [out]"
    - "No one can build off the table in playing cassino"
    - "In playing cassino the one claiming game first wins"
  - A 1922 Haskin column gave the standard order for counting out: "Cards, spades, Big Cassino, Little Cassino, aces and sweeps. If the aces have to decide the game, they score in the order of spades, clubs, hearts and diamonds" [S134].
- **Jack London, "A Goboto Night" (*A Son of the Sun*, 1912)** is the best literary transcript of Cassino table talk [S28]:
  - Opening challenge and taunt: "There's a little game called casino—I wonder if you ever heard of it?—a child's game." / "Afraid of a little game like casino … I'll play you for pennies—or farthings."
  - Agreeing house rules before the deal: "Do you count sweeps?" / "Certainly not … That's a sissy game." / "I'm glad … I don't like sissy games either."
  - Settling the counting-out order: "Cards and spades go out first, of course, and then big and little casino, and the aces in the bridge order of value. Is that right?"
  - Cutting: "Low deals."
  - Card memory and needling at the last deal: "I suppose you can name the four cards I hold" / "The knave of spades, the deuce of spades, the tray of hearts, and the ace of diamonds." / "I can name only three of yours, a knave, an ace, and big casino." / "Wrong. There aren't five aces in the deck."
  - End-game claims: "I'll make 'cards' on you. That's all I need." / "I'll let you save little casino … and still I'll make 'cards' and go out with big casino. Play." / "No 'cards' and I win! … I go out on little casino and the four aces. 'Big casino' and 'spades' only bring you to twenty." / "Count again."
- **Harper's Bazaar (29 Sep 1883), a tie on "cards":** an Albany father: "The cards are a tie, Katy, so neither of us takes that point." The daughter: "let's count it. You may have it. I feel just like one of the women that brought the child before Solomon. I'd rather you'd take it than to have it worth nothing to either of us" [S135].
- **Exclamations over Big Cassino** (fiction, FTS snippet): "she pointed to big cassino, butted her finger on big cassino, and screamed the word, 'Luck!'"; "You would have big cassino for the last. Such luck!" (Jane K. Ardmore, *To Love Is to Listen*, 1967) [S82].
- **"Big cassino in another game."** A woman at a poker table lifts the top card, "the ten of diamonds", and "murmured with a little laugh in a stage whisper: 'Big cassino in another game.'" (*Daily Kennebec Journal*, 16 Aug 1910) [S136]

### 4.3 Table talk & terms in other languages
- **Swedish (Kasino)** [S3][S4][S85]:
  - *tabbe* (plural *tabbar*): the sweep.
  - *sistan* ("the last [one]"): the point for the last capture. The dealer warns of the final deal by saying **"sistan" (last) or "båt" (boat)**.
  - *storan* / *lillan*: the two counting cards.
  - *Krypkasino*: "playing a card which does not capture anything is known as *krypa* (creeping or crawling)". A sweep there is a penalty, with *dubbeltabbe* (double sweep).
  - *mulle*: a card capturing its identical twin, in the double-deck *Mulle*.
  - *överspader*: a variant scoring each spade above six.
- **Norwegian (Kasino)** [S86]:
  - *tabbe*: sweep. *bygge*: to build. *doble* a build: to double it.
  - "Lokale navn finnes også, for eksempel kan spillet kalles tabbe og siste runde kalles 'døgga'" ("There are local names too: the game can be called *tabbe*, and the last round *døgga*").
  - Reverse variant: *Smyg* / *krypkasino*.
- **Danish (Kasino)** [S87]:
  - A sweep "kaldes det en 'svupper'" ("is called a *svupper*").
  - The dealer marks the last round "ved at udmelde 'sidsten'" ("by announcing 'sidsten'", i.e. "the last").
  - Builders call out **"bygger 9"** ("building 9").
  - A pair build is declared **"mere syv"** ("more seven").
  - House rule: "Spar 5 rydder bordet" ("the five of spades clears the table").
- **Finnish (Kasino)** [S3][S88][S92]:
  - A sweep is a **mökki** ("hut/cottage"). Wiktionary also lists *mökki* as the Finnish for the casino sweep [S9].
  - When an opponent already has a *mökki*, your sweep "kaadetaan" ("topples") one of theirs: "kaadetaan yksi vastapuolen mökeistä" [S92].
  - Tied points "jää pakkaan" ("stay in the deck") and carry over to the next deal [S88].
  - Build calls in *rakennuskasino*: **"rakennan ässälle"** ("I'm building for the ace", i.e. 14), **"rakennan yhdeksikölle"** ("building for the nine"), **"rakennan kuutoselle"** ("building for the six") [S92].
  - Etiquette: "pelaajan on oltava tarkkana ja sanottava, mitä tekee … pitää sanoa selvästi rakentaako kolmoselle vai kuutoselle" ("the player must be careful and say what he is doing … must say clearly whether he is building threes or a six") [S92].
  - Other variants: *laistokasino* (misère, avoid points) [S88][S92]; **pimeä kasino** ("dark cassino"), where "pelaaja ei valitse pelattavaa korttia vaan kääntää … aina päällimmäisen" ("the player doesn't choose a card but always turns the top one"). It is a luck game "johon perheen pienimmätkin voivat osallistua" ("that even the smallest family members can join") [S92].
- **Hungarian (Kaszinó)** [S5][S89]:
  - *csere* or *ütés*: a capture.
  - "táblát csinált" ("made a table"): a sweep, also called *söprés* ("sweeping").
  - Going out: "Amint a játékos már tudja, hogy elérte a 11 pontot, akkor ezt '**Ausz!**' vagy '**Kint vagyok!**' felkiáltással jelzi" ("As soon as the player knows he has reached 11, he signals it by exclaiming 'Ausz!' or 'Kint vagyok!' ['I'm out!']"). If he fails to declare and the opponent declares first, the opponent wins.
  - Pagat adds the form "**Ausz vagyok!**" [S5].
  - Challenge call: "a '**Fals!**' szóval jelzi, hogy ellenőrzést kér" ("with the word 'Fals!' he signals that he wants a check"). A wrong capture costs 2 points to the challenger's benefit, and a false challenge gives 1 to the capturer.
  - "passz": after a sweep the opponent may say this to waive the forced double turn.
  - Majority of spades is "pikkje van" [S5].
  - Kaszinó "a 20. század elején népszerű volt Magyarországon" ("was popular in Hungary at the start of the 20th century") [S89].
- **Dominican Republic (Royal Casino)** [S5]:
  - Cards that could have been captured but were left are "dejado" (left behind) or "pisado" (stepped on).
  - "It is customary for the opponents to point out such cards."
- **Haiti:** "Casino, pronounced with the stress on the first syllable: *cásino*, is a popular card game in Haiti" [S5].
- **Southern Africa (Swazi/Sotho/South African Casino)** [S6]:
  - "the capture or 'chow'"
  - "drifting": playing without capturing
  - "crazy casino": an 8-card draw variant
  - Namibia (Oshiwambo): *Omulongo Womadi*
- **German:** after a sweep the next player must "eine Karte für den Tisch bringen" ("bring a card for the table") [S91].
- **Japanese:** カシノ ("kashino"). The article compares it to hanafuda's *hana-awase* and *koi-koi* [S90].

### 4.4 Calls in a novelty variant
- **Tuxedo** (American, played with Rook cards): "An 'orange' is scored when a player captures a red and a yellow card … The 'orange' must be called by the player in order to score. If the player forgets, another player can score 10 points by calling 'orange'. A false call of 'orange' incurs a 10 point penalty" [S5].
  - This is a good model for a "call it or lose it" mechanic in a video game.

### 4.5 Superstitions, luck talk, and fatalism
- *Birmingham Age-Herald*, 18 Mar 1912, a premonition story [S137]:
  - A man won game after game at cassino ("at $1 a corner") for a week, then "slapped his cards down and said: 'Boys, I'm going to die.'"
  - "They tried to laugh him out of it, but he was fatalistic."
  - "I shall cash before long"
- Finnish house-rule commentary blames luck: with bad luck one player can collect several *mökki* in one hand, "eikä ensimmäinen pelaaja voi muuta kuin kiroilla onneaan" ("and the first player can do nothing but curse his luck"). Hence a house rule of one *mökki* per hand [S92].
- Religious objection and its workaround (Finland, 2016 comment): "kaikki eivät hyväksy neljän kuninkaan raamattua" ("not everyone accepts the 'Bible of the four kings'", i.e. playing cards). The commenter still recommends Kasino for teaching children addition [S92].
- Sermonising with cassino (*Lyon County Times*, Silver City, Nev., 8 Aug 1875). A preacher says: "The people of this world are like a deck of cards. There is now and then an ace; once in a while you come upon big cassino, and now and then some little scrub turns out to be little cassino; but though the pasteboard in the rest is just as fine … they don't count" [S138].

## 5. Figurative & slang uses (verified)

### 5.1 "Big casino" (American slang)

3. **"the best, the ultimate, the most important"**:

### 5.3 "Cards and spades" ("to give someone cards and spades")
- Meaning: "a liberal handicap", as in "could give him *cards and spades* and still beat him at his own game" (Merriam-Webster) [S14].
- Origin: cassino scoring, where "you count both cards and spades in your tricks". Jonathon Green on languagehat [S13]:
  - Earliest citation 1861: "Col. Crook could give cards and spades and then learn them something".
  - 1888: "Artie found a Chinaman out in 'Frisco who could give him cards and spades and beat him out".
  - Green "cannot establish a definitive link" to the game, though it "likely originates from cassino".
- Extended forms from fetched newspapers and fiction:
  - "England … has its full quota of aunties who fondly imagine that they can give the government cards and spades and little and big cassino and then beat the military authorities out in the game of war" (*Red Lodge Picket*, 5 Jan 1900) [S145].
  - "any man with a corn, could give me cards and spades and big Cassino and then beat me out when it comes to knowing things about the weather" (*Evening Star*, 27 Apr 1901) [S146].
  - The chief bartender of the St. Regis: someone "could still give [him] cards, spades, big and little cassino and one ace and beat him at the game of mixing" (*Sauk Centre Herald*, 14 Dec 1905) [S147].
  - "Twelve per cent of maximum efficiency gives the steam engine cards and spades and big cassino and then beats it!" (*Norwich Bulletin*, 22 Nov 1913) [S148].
  - Jack London, *The Valley of the Moon* (1913), on marriage: "We can give 'em cards and spades an' little casino an' win out on big casino and the aces" [S29].
  - Stephen Crane 1896, via GDoS (above) [S10].
  - "Mother" Jones (Chicago *Day Book*, 21 May 1913): "West Virginia could give cards and spades to any bloody ruler in the world" [S167].
  - Objection to the idiom: an opponent wrote that a rival "can give St. Peter 'cards and spades' and beat him denying every day in the week". A North Carolina paper protested: "the writer cannot express his idea except by reference to the card table. St. Peter a liar! St. Peter with cards and spades!" (*Goldsboro Weekly Argus*, 1 Nov 1900) [S167].
- **Counting-out disputes behind the idiom.** Jack London's player says "Cards and spades go out first, of course" [S28]. A letter signed "Old Casino" (*Pioche Weekly Record*, Nev., 6 Sep 1890) disagrees. It quotes the *New York Clipper*: "There have been several 'Hoyles' on cards, but no one that we ever saw has said that cards and spades go out first at casino. No one point has precedence over another not otherwise agreed upon" [S167]. [My inference, not stated by any source: "cards and spades" were the two majority points that the count usually started from, which may explain why the pair became shorthand for a big head start.]

### 5.3b "Sweep" as figurative cassino imagery
- *Lancaster Daily Intelligencer*, 1 Apr 1885, on a saloon's free lunch: "by 11 o'clock the plates looked as [if] those in attendance had been playing cassino with them and made a sweep at every play" [S163].
- *Mineral Point Tribune*, 1878: "little and big cassino make a clean sweep. It's hard on those who get swept" [S102].

### 5.4 "Little Cassino" as a battlefield / place nickname
These are not from the card game. They borrow the WWII battle of Monte Cassino, but they show the word's afterlife:
- Castelforte, Italy (May 1944), "whose almost impregnable position won it the name of 'Little Cassino'" [S149].
- Hill 140 (442nd RCT, 1944), "dubbed 'Little Cassino' by the men" [S149].
- A Korean peak taken by the all-Black 24th Infantry (Aug 1950), "named such by a veteran from the 92nd who fought at Cassino in Italy" [S149].
- Older US place names from the card game or the word:
  - "Big Cassino mill", Judith Basin, Mont. (1883) [S150]
  - "Big Cassino creek" and "Little Cassino Creek", Custer Co., Idaho (1900–06) [S150]
  - a "Little Cassino" business at White Oaks, Lincoln County, N.M. (1891) [S151]
  - the "Little Casino" grocery of Danziger, Railroad Avenue, Las Vegas, N.M. (1882) [S166]
  - the "Little Casino spring", Lewistown, Mont. (1902) [S166]
  - "the Little Cassino Restaurant" (Iowa, 1900) [S150]

## 7. National / regional status
- **USA:** the game's main home from about 1860 to 1950 (§3). Pratesi: "In America it finally lost many of its followers after the spread of games of … Rummy" [S7]. Scarne: popularity peaked "prior to the advent of Gin Rummy" [S7].
- **Britain:** fashionable craze in the 1790s London "Circles of Fashion" [S21][S7]. Regency drawing-room staple (§2). Seen as old-fashioned by the later 19th century [S7]. In Britain "Casino had the more traditional Cribbage as a rival" [S7].
- **German lands / Austria / Hungary:**
  - Rules in Vienna and Prague in 1795. A German "Royal"-style variant (courts 11–13, Great Cassino 10/16, Little 2/15) by 1810 [S1].
  - Wikipedia: the game "seems to have waned in Germany and Austria towards the end of the 19th century" [S1].
  - Pratesi: in Austria and Hungary new variants "entered the repertoire of ordinary players" [S7]. Kaszinó was popular in Hungary in the early 20th century [S89].
- **Nordic countries:**
  - Sweden: "I Sverige har kasino spelats sedan början av 1800-talet" ("In Sweden kasino has been played since the beginning of the 1800s") [S85].
  - Norway: "et populært kortspill" ("a popular card game") [S86].
  - Finland: "Kasino on hyvin suosittu peli. Se sopii lasten kanssa pelattavaksi, mutta myös aikuisille" ("Kasino is a very popular game. It suits playing with children, but also adults") [S92].
  - Pagat: the Nordic forms are Royal-style and "usually played without building" [S2][S3].
- **Southern Africa:** Swazi, Sotho, South African and Namibian forms with stealable capture piles [S6]. A Benedictine mission named "Cassino" in Nqutu, KwaZulu-Natal, is unrelated (it is named after Monte Cassino) [S49].
- **Caribbean:**
  - Dominican Republic: Royal Casino, anticlockwise, with "dejado/pisado" calls [S5].
  - Haiti: "cásino", "popular" [S5].
- **Brazil / Portuguese:** dictionaries define "Cassino grande/pequeno" for the counting cards [S83][S84], so the game was known. I found no evidence of how popular it was.
- **Newfoundland / Canada:** a heritage report on traditional Newfoundland and Labrador games does not mention cassino [S153]. A web search found Newfoundland card culture centred on 120s [S154]. Status: no evidence found (see Gaps).
- **Romania / Balkans:** Wikipedia says Tablanette "may have gained more traction in its various eastern European forms" [S1]. No Romanian Cassino evidence was fetched.

## 8. Physical aids & scoring customs found in cultural sources (for the game-design team)
- **"Cassino Markers of tortoiseshell"** in "a little box", bought in Russia in 1804 as a gift (Martha Wilmot) [S36]. Pairs well with the 1793 note on the three-handed game: "Six Counters are necessary to score with" [S21].
- **Visible sweep markers:**
  - Sweeps are kept as a card face-up in the capture pile [S2]. Rotated "at right angles to the rest of the pile so that the face up card is partly visible" in the Dominican game [S5].
  - Finnish *mökki* card turned "poikittain kuvapuoli ylöspäin" ("crosswise, face up") [S88].
  - Danish *svupper*: turn over the top card of one's trick pile [S87].
  - Hungarian: "Sweeps are recorded by drawing a line on the scoring slate/paper instead of keeping a card face up" [S5].
- **Cancelling sweeps** (one face-up sweep card turned down when both sides have one): Dominican [S5]. Finnish: a sweep "topples" the opponent's *mökki* [S88][S92].
- **Stakes vocabulary:**
  - "3d cassino" (1804) [S45]; "just for a trifle" (1797) [S20]
  - "$1 a corner" (1912) [S137]; "$25 a side" (1894) [S118]
  - "for a cigar or a bottle" (1901) [S119]
- **Progressive-party prize vocabulary:** "head prizes", "foot prizes", "consolation", "trophy of the evening" [S106][S109][S110].
- **Teaching variants worth prototyping:**
  - stick-cassino and "color-cassino" (1923) [S128]
  - Finnish *pimeä kasino* (blind play for small children) [S92]

## Gaps / leads not followed
- **OED:** the OED's actual first citation for the game (and for "Roberts 1792" and "Neres 1811") was not fetched; it is paywalled. These are known only through Pratesi [S7].
- **Trove (Australia):** the API needs a key ("No API key found in request"), so it was not searched.
- **Papers Past (NZ):** blocked by an Incapsula bot wall.
- **British Newspaper Archive:** not attempted.
- **HathiTrust full-text search:** not used. Could add more 1790s–1830s diaries.
- **Chronicling America:**
  - Phrase searches were run for "cassino party/parties/club", "big/little cassino", "game of cassino", "played cassino", "progressive cassino/casino". Hits were ALTO-OCR-checked.
  - Also run: "playing cassino", "royal cassino", "big casino", "little casino", "casino party", "cards and spades", "cassino sets", "cassino" + "sweep" (mostly WWII noise).
  - "Cassino tournament", "cassino contest" and "cassino champion(ship)" returned **zero** hits. Organised Cassino tournaments seem not to have been a newspaper phenomenon; "progressive" parties were the competitive format.
  - Only the first 80 results per query were OCR-checked, and the loc.gov API returns at most about 10 results for some phrase queries. Deeper paging would add more.
- **Pat Garrett & Billy the Kid "Big/Little Casino" nicknames:** not found in Burns 1926, the Siringo history, or Garrett's own 1882 *Authentic Life*. They may be a 20th-century invention, but this is unconfirmed. Next step: Lincoln County oral histories and 1870s–80s New Mexico newspapers.
- **Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, Romania, Brazil:** no substantive evidence found. Next steps: the Dictionary of Newfoundland English (site unreachable), MUN Folklore Archive, and Romanian sources for "casino" or *tablanet*.
- **Songs:** no song using big/little cassino as the card game was found.
- **TV:** only *The Sopranos* (via GDoS) is verified. Unchecked leads: *The Honeymooners*, *M\*A\*S\*H*, *The Waltons*, which should be checked in subtitle corpora.
- **Superstitions:** only one premonition anecdote was found [S137]. A targeted search of folklore journals (e.g., *Journal of American Folklore*, "cassino") could find more.
- **David Parlett's 2008 Penguin and *A History of Card Games* (1991) wording** on Cassino's origin was not read directly. Only the 1979/1987 Penguin text via FTS snippets [S50] and the Laugh-and-Lie-Down page [S51] were seen.

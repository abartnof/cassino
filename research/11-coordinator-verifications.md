# 11 — Coordinator verifications: resolving contradictions between the research notes

While combining notes 01–10, I found claims that disagreed with each other. I checked each one against the primary source, read directly from page images or OCR during this session (2 Oct 2026). The key page images are saved in `research/evidence/`.

---

## V1. Spelling of the earliest rules: "Cassino", not "Casino"
- **Conflict:** Pagat says "the earliest sources use the spelling Casino" [02 §A.1]. Pratesi (1995) gives Long's title as "…Game of Casino" and calls the double-s "a printing mistake" [09 §2C]. Wikipedia says "Cassino" is used in the 1792 rules [01 §4].
- **Primary evidence:** the title page of Long (1792) reads, in display type, "SHORT RULES FOR PLAYING THE GAME OF *CASSINO*; BY ROBERT LONG", with imprint "PRINTED FOR J. OWEN, NO. 168, PICCADILLY; E. NEWBURY…; AND T. MORTIMER, TWICKENHAM. Entered at Stationer's-Hall" [V-S1, image `evidence/long1792_title.jpg`]. Page 5 has "Great Caſſino" and "Little Caſſino" [V-S1, `evidence/long1792_p5_the_game.jpg`].
- **Primary evidence for the rival spelling:** the 1793 *Casino; a mock-heroic poem* uses "Casino" throughout. It explicitly attacks Long's spelling: "Mr. R. L. speaks of Three-handed Casino, (or CASSINO as he erroneously spells it,)" [V-S2, lines ~765–766 of the OCR].
- **Resolution:** both spellings appear in the very first printed sources. Long (1792) uses **Cassino**; the 1792/93 poem uses **Casino** and calls Long's form an error. Pagat and Pratesi are wrong about Long's own title. Their broader point, that "Casino" was the competing early form, is correct. The 1796 Hoyle uses both, with running head "The Game of CASINO" and text "Cassino" [03 S5].

## V2. Did the earliest rules give a point for sweeps (clearing the board)? Yes.
- **Conflict:** Pratesi: early rules had no sweep points, and "The name itself of sweep was limited to the clearing of the table at the end of the game − bringing no points" [02 §B.8, 09 §4.1].
- **Primary evidence:**
  - **Long (1792), pp. 6–7:** "When any one matches a Card in his Hand with *all* the Cards on the Board (as for Example, if he hold a Nine, and Six or Eight Cards on the Table should happen to make up Three or Four separate Nines), he clears the Board, and marks one Point in the Game as often as repeated." [V-S1, `evidence/long1792_p6.jpg`, `evidence/long1792_p7_sweep_point.jpg`]
  - **The 1793 poem, laws VI–VII:** "Whoever clears the Board reckons for each Time one Point absolutely." Then: "Whoever takes the last Trick, when all the Cards are dealt out, takes with it every Card that may remain on the Board, and this is called the Sweep." [V-S2]
- **Resolution:** Pratesi is right about the *word*: in the poem tradition, and in Jones's Hoyle (1796), "The Sweep or last Trick", "Sweep" meant taking the leftover cards at the end [03 S5]. He is wrong about *points*: both 1792/93 sources score clearing the board mid-game as 1 point. It is left out of the "eleven points" table, which is why it is easy to miss. The modern meaning of "sweep" (a mid-game clearance worth 1) is first seen in Jones's 1800 text: "Besides a sweep before the end of the game, when a player can match all on the board, reckons 1" [03 S6].

## V3. Two rival rule traditions in 1792–93, and which one went to the Continent
- **The Long tradition (London, 1792):**
  - Lurch = 6: "Six Points gained save the Lurch" [V-S1, p. 5].
  - The proto-call: hold a pair, lay one down and wait [03 S1].
  - Three-handed: the two lowest scores combined are deducted from the highest [03 S1].
  - This text continues in Pigott (1795) and the British/American Hoyles: lurch 6 [03 S2, S5].
- **The poem tradition (London, 1792/93):**
  - "The Lurch is Five" [V-S2].
  - The author says the "Custom of the first Clubs and the first Circles in London will decide against" Long's lurch of six [V-S2].
  - Three-handed is played to 15 with no deduction, "Six Counters are necessary to score with", and the lurch is 7 [V-S2].
  - **The earliest count-out ("seniority") order:** "Great Casino is 1st to be reckoned. Little Casino 2d. The Cards 3d. Majority of Spades 4th. Ace of Spades 5th. Ace of Clubs 6th. Ace of Hearts 7th. Ace of Diamonds the last." The rule is explicitly modelled on All-Fours and Piquet: "the Game is to be decided by the Seniority of the Points they have gained, in the same Manner as at All-Fours" [V-S2, p. 31, image `evidence/casino_poem1793_p31_seniority.jpg`].
  - It also derives the name from "D'Alberti's Dictionary … Espece d'Academie de jeu de la Noblesse a Florence" [04 S21].
- **The German translation (1797)** has, per note 10:
  - "Lurch (Bredouille)" = **five**;
  - three-handed to **15** with the order "Erstlich das große Casino, 2tens das kleine Casino, 3tens die Mehrheit der Karten, 4tens die Mehrheit der Piques, 5tens das Pique-As, 6tens das Trefle-As, 7tens das Coeur-As, 8tens das Caro-As";
  - the "Spielhause in Florenz" naming story;
  - "Sweep oder Kehraus" for the last cards [10 §7].

  The Polish 1821 and Danish 1829 texts repeat it: Danish "Lurchen … er Fem"; Polish "Sweep albo wymiatacz" plus the Florence story [10 §5, §8].
- **Inference (mine, from the matching details above):** the Continental Casino literature descends from the **poem/"Casino" tradition**, not from Long. The shared markers are lurch 5, three-handed to 15 with the same seniority order, and the Florentine etymology. This would explain why German sources "invariably use the spelling 'Casino'" [01 §4]. It also suggests that Pratesi's view of early rules (spelling Casino; "sweep" = final clearance) was formed from this tradition.
- **Design relevance:** a "count-out order" (tie-breaking when several players pass the target) is attested from 1793 [V-S2]. It was still disputed in the 1880s–1910s: Foster puts cards first [03 S31]; a *New York Clipper* reply says "No one point has precedence" [04 S131, S167]; Jack London's characters say "Cards and spades go out first, of course" [04 S28]. A game needs to pick one and show it.

## V4. When did Cassino, building and calling enter American print? The 1864 claim is wrong.
- **Conflict:** Wikipedia: "Trumps" 1864 had the first "building up" [01 §2.1, 02 §D.4]. Note 05 cites "the 1864 *American Hoyle*" for the audibility rule. Note 09 gives "copyright 1864, 4th ed.". Pratesi gives New York 1866.
- **Primary evidence:**
  - The archive.org copy `americanhoyleorg00dick` is the **fourth edition**. Its "PREFACE TO THE FOURTH EDITION" says: "Among several important card games which have been added to the fourth edition, the following may be particularly specified; Cassino, Commercial Pitch, … Rounce; …" [V-S3, OCR lines 134–166]. **Cassino was therefore not in the first (1864) edition at all.** The 1864 date is the copyright of the first edition, carried forward on the verso.
  - Dick's *American Card Player* ("Entered according to Act of Congress, in the year 1866") has the earliest building text found: "Building Up.—Suppose the dealer's four cards in hand to be … This is called building up" [V-S4, OCR lines 402, 7155–7162].
- **Resolution:** building is first in print in **1866** (*American Card Player*). Building with full laws plus **calling** (plural announcements) is first in the **4th edition of the *American Hoyle*** (preface dated Feb. 1867) [03 S15]. "1864" should not be cited for either.

## V5. "Set-back or Rounce Cassino", not "Bounce"
- **Conflict:** notes 01, 05 and 09 write "Bounce Cassino". Note 03 writes "Rounce".
- **Primary evidence:**
  - The page image of the 4th-ed. *American Hoyle*, p. 221, Law 14 note: "The European game is the favorite with those who play merely for recreation, and is known as Set-back or Rounce Cassino." [V-S3, `evidence/americanhoyle_4thed_p221_rounce.jpg`]
  - The 1868 *Modern Pocket Hoyle* OCR reads "Rounce, or Set-Back Cassino" and "Set-Back or Rounce Cassino" [V-S5].
  - "Rounce" is also a separate game newly added in the same 4th edition [V-S3].
- **Resolution:** "Bounce" is an OCR misreading (another scan reads "flounce"). Use **Rounce**.

## V6. Earliest "Big/Little Cassino" in American English
- **Conflict:** note 05: "Big Cassino" is first in Foster 1897. Note 03: Townsend 1891 ("Big Casino").
- **Evidence from note 04** (Chronicling America):
  - *Mineral Point Tribune* (Wis.), 8 May 1878: "They play it now where little and big cassino make a clean sweep" [04 S102].
  - "Little Cassino" as a dime-novel hero, *New York Saturday Journal*, 1876 [04 S142].
  - "Big Cassino mill", Montana, 1883 [04 S150].
  - A sermon, 1875: "once in a while you come upon big cassino, and now and then some little scrub turns out to be little cassino" [04 S138].
- **Resolution:** "big/little cassino" was in American speech by **1875**, well before it appears in rulebooks (Townsend 1891; Foster 1897). The rulebooks kept "Great Cassino" longer than speakers did.

## V7. Spade Cassino points per hand: 24, not 25
- Foster (1897) says "24 points are made in every hand, exclusive of sweeps" [01 §2.1 (S78); 03 S23]. Pagat's breakdown is "cards (3) + ten of diamonds (2) + the four aces and the two and jack of spades (6) + the spades (13)" = 24 [02 §A.5]. The new Italian Wikipedia's "25" is an error [01 §2.8].

## V8. Royal Cassino: older than its usual attribution
- In substance (J 11, Q 12, K 13) it appears in an 1846 English report of German play [03 S11], in the 1810 German *Spielalmanach* [01 §2.1 (S83)], and in Dick's 1894 American "Interesting Variation… now very generally played" [03 S22].
- Under the name "Royal Cassino" it appears in Foster 1897 [03 S23].
- "Royal Draw Cassino" is in print by 1898 [03 S24], not first in 1911 as Wikipedia says.

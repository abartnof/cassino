# 03 — Historical rulebooks and game manuals for Cassino (1792–1952)

Scope: printed rules and manual treatments of Cassino/Casino from the first known rules (1792) to the early 1950s. All primary texts below were fetched in this session, either as Project Gutenberg plain text or as Internet Archive OCR (`https://archive.org/download/<id>/<id>_djvu.txt`), and grepped. Robert Long's 1792 pamphlet was read from the page images (`https://archive.org/download/<id>/page/n<N>_s4.jpg`), because its OCR is unusable. Where a quote's spelling looks odd, it comes from OCR. Long-s (ſ) has been normalised to "s". Page numbers are the book's printed page numbers as they appear in the OCR running heads.

Citation keys `[S#]` are listed in **Sources** at the end.

## 0. Key findings

- **The earliest rules (1792) had no building and no calling.** Cards were captured only by pairing or by "equalling any number of Pips". Court cards could only pair. Long gives a sweep point ("clears the Board, and marks one Point in the Game") but leaves it out of his 11-point table [S1].
- **Long already describes a proto-call, but nothing is said aloud.** With a pair in hand and a third card of that rank on the board, you "may (if he pleases) lay down one of them and wait his Turn", then take all three. However, "he must not cover the Trick by putting the Cards together; the Adversary being at Liberty to take them if he can" [S1]. Pigott (1795) [S2] and the "Hoyle's Improved Edition" line [S9] copy this.
- **Two separate lines of text dominated the British and early American Hoyles for about 80 years without adding building:**
  - Line A: Charles Jones's *Hoyle's Games Improved* (1796 [S5], 1800 [S6], then 1814/1835/1859 and Bohn's *Hand-book of Games*, 1850/1867 [S12]).
  - Line B: Pigott 1795 [S2], then *Hoyle's Games Improved* (New York, 1823) [S8], then *Hoyle's Improved Edition* (1830/1838) [S9], then Philadelphia editions of 1857/1869 [S13], Frere (Boston, 1875) [S17] and Pardon (London, 1876) [S18].
  - None of these texts mentions building. Grep for "build" finds nothing in their Cassino sections.
- **Building and calling are American additions by William Brisbane Dick ("Trumps").**
  - The *American Card Player* (1866) has "Building up" but no calling [S14].
  - The 4th edition of the *American Hoyle* (preface dated February 1867) has full laws of **building and calling**, with the singular/plural announcement convention: build "Nine", call "Fives" [S15].
  - Cassell's English compendium (c. 1881) copies Dick's glossary word for word [S19].
  - Baxter-Wray's English *Round Games with Cards* (1891/1897) still has no building at all [S21].
- **What players say aloud is a defined, rule-governed part of the game from 1867.**
  - Dick: "the player must declare the denomination of the proposed 'build' or 'call,' audibly and distinctly", or else the opponent "may separate the cards" [S15].
  - Townsend (1891) adds explicit verbs, "I build seven" and "I call sixes", "to avoid the disputes caused by misunderstanding whether singular or plural number was called" [S20].
  - Mid-20th-century rules use "building six" / "building fours" [S36] and "Building eight" / "Building sevens" [S35].
  - Foster uses "Two Sevens" and "Two Nines" for doubled builds [S31].
- **Royal Cassino is older than Foster's 1897 *Complete Hoyle*,** which Wikipedia [S38] names as its first appearance. Dick's 1894 *American Hoyle* (15th ed.) already gives "An Interesting Variation… now very generally played", with Knave 11, Queen 12 and King 13 [S22].
  - The USPC 1898 booklet adds aces as 1 or 14. It also proposes 60-card packs with 11s and 12s, which make Jacks 13, Queens 14, Kings 15 and aces 1 or 16 [S24].
  - The *New International Encyclopaedia* (1905) adds "the 'joker' fifteen" [S27].
- **"Royal Draw Cassino" is in print by 1898** [S24], earlier than the 1911 source Wikipedia cites [S38]. Plain "Draw Cassino" is in Foster by 1909 [S29].
- **Diamond Casino is in print by 1945/49 (Ostrow), not only in modern Parlett** [S35]:
  - 40 cards ("the tens, nines and eights are stripped");
  - 3 cards to each hand, 4 to the table;
  - scoring: 1 for most cards (27 or more), 1 for most diamonds (7 or more), 1 for the 7♦, 2 for all four 7s, 6s or Aces, 1 per sweep;
  - game 11.

  Ostrow also gives "Spade Royal", "overs" and a "going-out declaration" [S35].
- **"Can a player raise his own build?" was disputed for 80 years:**
  - No: 1866 [S14], 1898 [S24], 1904 [S25].
  - Yes, if he holds cards for both the old and new build: Foster 1897–1914, who calls the opposite view "a common error" [S31]; also 1907 [S28], 1929 [S33], 1949 [S35] and 1952 [S36].
- **The modern "only one court card at a time" rule appears in the 20th century** [S35][S36]. In 1792 court cards "can only take up as many of the same Kind as are on the Board" [S1]. Foster's advice was that "It is considered bad policy to take in three court cards, as it stops all sweeps when the fourth appears" [S31].
- **Spelling and origin claims:**
  - "Cassino" is the spelling from 1792 onward [S1].
  - "Casino" appears in Townsend 1891 [S20], the *Standard Hoyle* 1904 [S25], Fox 1905 [S26] and Ostrow ("Sometimes spelled cassino") [S35].
  - Dick (1866) asserts "Cassino is of Italian origin" [S14]. Foster calls Cassino the game "typical" of America [S31].
- **Decline in England:** Professor Hoffmann's *Hoyle's Games Modernized* (London, 1909) has no Cassino at all [S30].

## 2. Source-by-source notes (chronological)

### [S1] Robert Long, *Short Rules for Playing the Game of Cassino* (London, 1792): the first known printed rules
- Imprint: "PRINTED FOR J. OWEN, NO. 168, PICCADILLY; E. NEWBURY, THE CORNER OF ST. PAUL'S CHURCH-YARD; AND T. MORTIMER, TWICKENHAM. Entered at Stationer's-Hall." British Museum copy, shelfmark 1040.a.12(?). The pamphlet is 13 images long [S1].
- **Strategy rules, p. 3–4.** Verbatim:
  - "I. Take up the Card played by your Adversary in Preference to any other."
  - "II. Take up Spades in Preference to the other Suits."
  - "III. When you hold a Pair, play one of them."
  - "IV. Aim at clearing the Board always, but forego an Advantage rather than give your Enemy that Chance."
  - "V. Never play a Ten while Great Cassino is in, nor a Deuce while Little Cassino is unplayed."
  - "VI. Try to win the last Trick."
  - "VII. Try to win the Majority of the Cards, and take up as many as you can with one Card."
  - "VIII. When obliged to lay down a Card, let it be a Court-Card, or a little one, but keep the Aces if possible."
  - "IX. If you have a Pair, and a Third is on the Board, play one; and wait your Turn: you may take any thing else in the mean Time." [S1]
- **The Game, p. 5:** "THE GAME consists of Eleven Points, viz: The Ten of Diamonds, which is Great Cassino, marks two Points: The Deuce of Spades, which is Little Cassino, marks one Point: The Majority of the Cards — three Points. The Majority of the Spades — one Point: and The Four Aces — one Point each." Under "THE LURCH": "Six Points gained save the Lurch." [S1]
- **Dealing, p. 6:** "Deal Four Cards each, turning up Four alternately upon the Board (the first Deal only), after which deal Four Cards each, without any for the Board, till the Pack is out." [S1]
- **Playing, p. 6–7:**
  - "always taking from the Table as many Cards as will answer to the Number of Pips on the Card you lay down; then face them like Tricks before you."
  - Sweep: "When any one matches a Card in his Hand with *all* the Cards on the Board (as for Example, if he hold a Nine, and Six or Eight Cards on the Table should happen to make up Three or Four separate Nines), he clears the Board, and marks one Point in the Game as often as repeated." [S1]
- **Trailing, without the word, p. 7:** "When the Player cannot take up a Card, either by pairing or equalling any number of Pips on the Board with a Card in his Hand, he must lay down a Card and wait his Turn: in this Case he ought to lay down the Court-Cards in Preference to the others, because they can only take up as many of the same Kind as are on the Board, and are less profitable than the Eights, Nines, Tens, &c." [S1]
- **Proto-call, p. 7–8:** "When the Player holds Two Cards of a Kind, and a Third should happen to be on the Table, he may (if he pleases) lay down one of them and wait his Turn; and should the Adversary not match it, he may lay down the Third and take up the former Two with it: but, in this Case, he must not cover the Trick by putting the Cards together; the Adversary being at Liberty to take them if he can. — N.B. This ought only to be done when the Fourth Card is out. While the first Pair are so laid, the Player is at Liberty to take any Thing else that offers, and to lay down his third Card when he pleases." [S1]
- **Four-handed, p. 8–9:** played "with Partners like the Game of Whist, and is subject to its Laws in cutting for Places, dealing, and marking the Games single or double; and playing the Rubber, which is gaining Two Points out of Three." [S1]
- **Three-handed, p. 9:** "each Party marks separately, and in counting up the Points of the Game, the *two lowest* join their Numbers together, and deduct them from *the greater*". Example: A 5, B 4, C 2, so A "can mark Nothing" [S1].
- **Laws for counting the game, p. 10–11:**
  - Last cards: unmatched cards go to "the Person who took up last … but this does not intitle him to mark a Point, as in the Middle of the Game."
  - Ties on cards: "neither Party can mark any Thing for them".
  - Difference scoring: "the lesser Number must be deducted from the greater (for Example, if A has Seven Points and B Four, A marks Three and B none)." [S1]
- **Of placing the cards, p. 11.** This is an early "physical handling" rule: "To prevent Mistakes, the Dealer should make his Partner take up the Tricks and separate the Cards, by dividing the Pairs and placing one at the Top and the other at the Bottom of the Tricks, that they may not come in Pairs next Deal." [S1]
- Memory and endgame, p. 11: "N.B. It is recommended to the Players to remember the Cards played, as no one can be permitted to refer to the Tricks. In the last Deal, a Court-Card or some other ought to be kept to secure the Advantage of the Cards on the Board." [S1]
- Wikipedia also cites a reprint, "Short Rules for Playing the Game of Cassino", in *The Conjuror's Magazine*, January 1793 [S38]. **[UNVERIFIED — not fetched]**

### [S2] Charles Pigott, *Pigott's New Hoyle, or the General Repository of Games* (London, 1795)
- Cassino appears as an **"ADDENDA"** at the end of the book, after "FINIS". Its printed pages run up to about p. 443, where All Fours follows. Its position suggests the game was a late addition [S2].
- Terms:
  - "Great Cassino is the ten of diamonds, and reckons for two points … The cards is having a greater share of the pack than your adversary, and reckons for three points."
  - "Lurched is, when your adversary has won the game before you have gained six points." [S2]
- Rules and maxims, paraphrasing Long:
  - "When you can neither pair, or take up any cards, play such a card as will not assist to make up an eight, nine, or ten, &c. In this case, the best is to play a pictured card, or a small one, but not an ace."
  - "When you hold a pair, and a similar card is on the table, you should, if the fourth is out, (it is not judicious otherwise) lay down one of them, wait your turn to play the other … N.B. Your antagonist may take them, if he can."
  - "Though you can play your cards to advantage, avoid doing it, when it may give your opponent an opportunity of clearing the board." [S2]
- Penalty: "In case you are lurched, you lose a double stake, provided you do not agree to the contrary before you commence the game." Also: "ELEVEN POINTS CONSTITUTE THE GAME." [S2]
- In the 1797 edition [S3], Cassino has moved into the main body, at about pp. 398–399. The index entry reads "CASSINO, the game of, described" [S3].

### [S4] Frederick Reynolds, *Cheap Living* (comedy, Drury Lane, 1797): Cassino as a fashionable craze
- Lines from the play: "I do long for a game of cassino — that is, in the family way — just for a trifle." / "your insatiable passion for cassino, hazard, …" / "time is so much better employed in playing cassino" [S4].

### [S5] Charles Jones (ed.), *Hoyle's Games Improved*, "A New Edition Enlarged" (London: R. Baldwin et al., 1796), pp. 298–300
- The title page now lists "CASSINO". The 1790 *Hoyle's Games Improved* and the 1791 *Epitome of Hoyle* have no Cassino at all: grep of their full OCR returns 0 hits [S39].
- "CASSINO is generally played by four People, but occasionally by three or two; the Points consist of eleven, and the Lurch is six." One table line reads "The Sweep or last Trick" [S5].
- Scoring: "the smaller Number is constantly subtracted from the larger both in Tricks and Points, and if they both prove equal, the Game commences again" [S5].
- Dealer "lays in any Manner he likes, four more Face upwards on the Board" [S5].
- Maxims new in Jones:
  - "The principal objects are to remember how many Tens, Aces, and Deuces have been played, and to clear the Hand of Court Cards, which are only of Service in pairing or in gaining the Sweep."
  - "When three Aces are out, take the first Opportunity to play the fourth … it is better even to play the little Cassino than to risk the Ace that may be paired by the Opponent, and make a difference of two Points."
  - "But when any Player holds an Ace and has reason to imagine the Antagonist has neither Ace nor Nine, then 'tis advisable to play that Ace."
  - "When the Deuces are out, an eight is an eligible Lead; and when the Trays, a Seven, as they cannot then be combined."
  - "Attend to the Adversaries Score, and if possible prevent them from saving their Lurch."
  - "At the Commencement of a Game combine all the Cards possible, for that is more difficult than pairing" [S5].
- The running head on p. 299 reads "The Game of CASINO". This is an early "Casino" spelling inside a "Cassino" text [S5].

### [S6] Jones, *Hoyle's Games Improved*, new edition (London, 1800), pp. 296–298
- The table now reads: "Besides a sweep before the end of the game, when a player can match all on the board, reckons 1" [S6].
- Added maxims:
  - "Do not neglect sweeping the board when opportunity offers; always prefer taking up the card laid down by the opponent, also as many as possible with one, endeavouring likewise to win the last cards."
  - "While great or little cassino is in, avoid playing either a ten or a deuce."
  - "When you hold a pair, lay down one of them, unless when there is both a similar card on the table, and the fourth is not yet out."
  - "Attend to the adversaries score … even though you otherwise seemingly get less yourself, particularly if you can hinder them from clearing the board." [S6]
- The "eight is an eligible lead" maxim has been dropped [S6].
- The same text continues, with minor edits, in the 1814 Lowndes edition (pp. 159–162) [S7], the 1835 Longman *Hoyle's Games Improved and Enlarged* (p. 244–246) [S10] and Bohn [S12].

### [S8]/[S9]/[S13] Line B: *Hoyle's Games Improved* (New York: G. Long, 1823); *Hoyle's Improved Edition of the Rules for Playing Fashionable Games* (New York: W. C. Borradaile, 1830; 1838); *Hoyle's Games* (Philadelphia: H. F. Anners, 1857; J. B. Lippincott, 1869)
- Heading: "Terms used in the Game of Cassino". "The Game of Cassino is played with an entire pack of cards, generally by four persons, but sometimes by three, and often by two" [S9].
- 16 numbered "rules for playing". Examples:
  - "4. Always remember to take up spades in preference to any other suit."
  - "5. Always endeavour to remember the cards played and those which remain in."
  - "13. Even if you should have it in your power to play your cards to advantage, nevertheless avoid doing it, when it may give your adversary an opportunity of clearing the board."
  - "14. When you happen to take up a pair, always endeavour to separate them … in order to prevent their coming in pairs the next deal." [S9]
- Penalty: "If you are lurched, you lose a double stake" [S9].

### [S11] Lady Sarah Nicolas (comp.), *The Cairn: a Gathering of Precious Stones from Many Hands* (London, 1846), p. 128
- Quote: "The game of Cassino is thus played in some parts of Germany:— Great Cassino takes sixteen. Little Cassino—fifteen. Every Ace—fourteen. King—thirteen. Queen—twelve. Knave—eleven." [S11]

### [S12] H. G. Bohn (ed.), *The Hand-book of Games* (London: Bohn, 1850; Bell & Daldy, 1867), pp. 330–332
- Bohn reprints the Jones text (Line A). He adds the three-hand rule: "but when their two numbers together either amount to or exceed the highest, then neither party scores" [S12].
- Maxim: "if great cassino and an ace be on the board prefer the ace, as it may be paired or combined, but great cassino can only be paired." [S12]

### [S14] W. B. Dick, *The American Card Player* (New York: Dick & Fitzgerald, ©1866), pp. 128–130 and 151
- Origin claim: **"Cassino is of Italian origin, and is a pleasant, simple game, when the stakes played for are not too high."** [S14]
- **Earliest building text fetched in this session:** "Building Up.—Suppose the dealer's four cards in hand to be a six [sic], ten, and two aces—his adversary plays a six,—the dealer puts an ace upon it and says 'seven,' … the non-dealer throws a deuce upon them and says 'nine,' … the dealer again puts upon the heap his other ace, and cries 'ten,' … This is called building up." [S14]
- The rest is "given by Hoyle, and adopted by all his continuators", i.e. the Line A text, with 11-point difference scoring [S14].
- **Ruling on a disputed point, p. 151.** "A holds … a deuce, ace, nine and ten. He plays his deuce on a seven … making it nine; B … cannot take it. Can A play his ace and make it ten? Answer.—He cannot, but if B had played an ace upon it, he (A) might have taken it with his ten." [S14]

### [S15] "Trumps" (W. B. Dick), *The American Hoyle, or Gentleman's Hand-book of Games*, 4th ed. (New York: Dick & Fitzgerald; ©1864, preface dated Feb. 1867), pp. 218–221
- Glossary: Great/Little Cassino, The Cards, The Spades, The Aces, "The Sweep — matching all the cards on the board", Building up, Call, Build, Combine, Last Cards, Eldest Hand, Misdeal [S15].
- **Singular build vs. plural call:**
  - "It will be observed that a player, in announcing the denomination of a build, always employs the singular number. Thus: 'Nine' or 'Ten' — not 'Nines' or 'Tens.'"
  - "Call — Suppose a player to have in his hand two or more cards of the same denomination, and one or more cards of the same denomination remain upon the board, he may play one of them on the table, at the same time calling the denomination, and his opponent is thereby debarred from taking it with a card of any other denomination. In calling the denomination, the plural is always used. Thus: 'Fours,' not 'Four.'" [S15]
- Law 7 example: B "plays one of them, calling out (not Five, but) 'Fives.' A is debarred from taking any of them with a Ten"; "A … plays one of them, calling 'Treys'" [S15].
- Law 8: a builder "may not alter his build" (e.g. may not make his 7 build into 10). He must take it in, or make another build, pair or combine, "before playing a card which will not do either" [S15].
- Law 9 lets calls be stacked on one's own build: "A may play a Four from his hand on the top of the 'build' and call (not Four, but) 'Fours'… each of these Fours so played on a 'build' has all the immunities of a call." [S15]
- **Law 11, the announcement rule:** "When a card is played for the purpose of making a 'build,' or 'call,' the player must declare the denomination of the proposed 'build' or 'call,' audibly and distinctly, so that no doubt of his intentions may exist, and failing to comply with this requirement, his opponent may separate the cards, and employ them in any lawful way … the mere act of playing a Five on a Deuce does not of itself constitute a 'build,' … unless the player of the Five says … audibly and distinctly, 'Seven' … if he play a Five upon a Five on the table to make a 'call,' he must announce his intention by saying, clearly and audibly, 'Fives.'" [S15]
- Penalties: a false build or call "forfeits two points" (law 10). A misdeal forfeits the game [S15].
- Scoring: in America "the points gained by each party are counted at the end of each deal, and that party which has the greatest number of points wins the game". The old 11-point difference game is the "European" one: "The European game is the favorite with those who play merely for recreation, and is known as Set-back or Rounce Cassino." Both scans' index lines read "Rounce Cassino" [S15][S16].
- Maxims: the Line A maxims, revised to "At the commencement of a game, combine and build up all the cards possible" [S15].

### [S16] "Trumps", *The Modern Pocket Hoyle* (New York: Dick & Fitzgerald, ©1868), pp. 177–182
- Adds "Build from the Table.—Employing cards on the table to continue a build" and "False Build.—A build made without any card in hand to redeem it" [S16].
- Law 10: "A player cannot 'build' from the table. [For instance: if a Seven and Two are upon the table and a player put an Ace upon the Seven calling Eight, his opponent cannot employ the Two upon the table to 'build' it up to ten.]" [S16]
- Law 7 adds a combined call: put "the Four and Five on the table together, and play the Ace in hand upon the Eight on the table, putting them all together and calling 'Nines'" [S16].
- A false build "forfeits the game", justified at length. Points table: "great Cassino reckons … 2 points" [S16].

### [S17]/[S18] Thomas Frere, *Hoyle's Games* (Boston: De Wolfe, Fiske, 1875), pp. 334–336; G. F. Pardon, *The Card Player's Manual* (London: Ward, Lock, 1876), p. 231 ff.
- Both still print the no-building Line B text. Frere: "you should likewise give a preference to either of the cassinos or aces, though you may thereby take up fewer cards." [S17]
- Grep for "build" returns 0 in both books [S17][S18].

### [S19] *Cassell's Book of In-door Amusements, Card Games, and Fireside Fun* (London: Cassell, Petter, Galpin, [1881]; 3rd ed. on Gutenberg #49137), p. 129
- Opening: "In order to understand the simple but highly amusing game of Cassino it is absolutely necessary that a thorough knowledge of the technical terms used should be first acquired." [S19]

- Advice: "if he has a nine of hearts and a king of spades in his hand, and there is a nine and a king on the board, he should take the king in preference to the nine, as it will secure him a spade … Supposing a player to have in his hand a nine and a king, and there is a six and three and king on the board, he would do wisely to take the six and three, as that will help to a majority in cards." [S19]

### [S20] Charles Townsend, *Social Card Games* (Chicago: T. S. Denison, ©1891), pp. 89–94
- Spelled "CASINO". "Casino is a game depending more upon chance than skill, although a good memory is very necessary" [S20].
- **Table talk:**
  - "you play one saying: 'I call sixes,' and your adversary can only take it with a six."
  - "Building Up.—Suppose you hold ace, seven and nine. Your adversary plays a six, you put on the ace, saying, 'I build seven' (not sevens) your adversary puts on a two saying: 'I build nine'." [S20]
  - The reason for the explicit verbs: "if you say 'I call fours' you must hold a four and nothing but a four will take the call. On the contrary, if you say 'I build four' you leave the build open to further builds either by yourself or your adversary. The application of the words 'I build' and 'I call' has been made necessary to avoid the disputes caused by misunderstanding whether singular or plural number was called." [S20]
  - "In making a 'build' or a 'call' of any denomination, the words 'I build' or 'I call' must be spoken clearly." [S20]
- **"Progressive Building"**: build 5 to 8 calling "eight", then add a 2 to make ten. "In a build of this sort a player runs many risks … Toward the close of the game a careful player who remembers the fall of the cards may indulge in progressive building with a fair degree of success." [S20]
- Scoring and claims:
  - "the player who first scores twenty-one points wins the game";
  - sweep counts "(except to the winner of the last cards)";
  - "Cards won cannot be examined until the close of the hand, under the penalty of forfeiting two points for each offense";
  - "a player may announce twenty-one at any time, and if he can show that number, the game ends at once."
  - Partners "may build to aid each other although they may not hold the cards to redeem the build" [S20].

### [S21] "Baxter-Wray" (W. H. Peel), *Round Games with Cards* (London, 1891; 1897; Gutenberg #27819), pp. 97–100
- "This game, which is of considerable antiquity…". "It is usual to play with the full pack of fifty-two cards; there is however no reason why the smaller pack of thirty-two should not be used" [S21].
- **No building at all.** Last cards go to "the player who is last able to pair or combine … and scoring one point for thus 'sweeping the board,' as it is termed" [S21].
- Four-player independent scoring: the lowest scorer's points are deducted from each of the others [S21].
- Advice: "Secure the Cassino cards on the first opportunity, also aces and spades, after which aim to make as many combinations as possible, leaving the pairs until last, unless they be the ten or the two, which are always best got off the board as early as possible" [S21].

### [S22] "Trumps", *The American Hoyle*, 15th edition, "entirely re-written" (New York: Dick & Fitzgerald, 1894), pp. 316–322
- New vocabulary:
  - The table cards are "what is termed the 'lay-out'".
  - "The primary object in Cassino is to capture as many cards as possible, and this is done in five different ways: By Pairing, Combining, Building, Calling, and by Sweeps." [S22]
- On raising and combining:
  - "he may 'raise' the 'Build' by playing another card to it".
  - "Strictly speaking, the first Build is simply a Combination; if an adversary adds a card to the Combination, it is building … The player who combines can never build upon that Combination unless an opponent has previously built upon it." [S22]
- **"Calling, or Duplicating"**: "play one of the Nines from his hand upon the Nine … and call 'nines' (not nine); then nothing but a Nine will capture it." [S22]
- A subtle example: A plays an Ace on B's 8 build and, "provided he does not call 'nine', may add from the table a Six and a Three and call 'nines'… if A had said 'nine' when he played his Ace, this would have completed his play" [S22]. Here the spoken word has a mechanical effect: saying it ends the turn.
- **Sweep tally and cancellation.** This is a physical scoring affordance: "When a player makes a Sweep he turns the Sweep card … face upwards … If the opposing player makes a Sweep, these two Sweeps cancel each other, and the players turn the canceled Sweep cards down." [S22]
- **"An Interesting Variation. The following variation is now very generally played: … the players agree to count the Knave eleven, the Queen twelve, and the King thirteen."** This is Royal Cassino in substance, three years before Foster [S22].
- Twenty-One Point Cassino:
  - "Cassino is now very generally played for a fixed number of points (usually twenty-one)… points are scored as soon as made."
  - Players who turn sweeps down so that "a Sweep may not count a player out" are doing something "contrary to the spirit of the game".
  - A false claim to have won loses the game [S22].
- Four-handed: "the fact that he holds the necessary card to redeem it is sufficient authority for his partner to make a similar Build … without having a card of the same denomination in his hand" [S22].

### [S23]/[S29]/[S31] R. F. Foster, *Foster's Complete Hoyle* (New York: F. A. Stokes, 1897, pp. 441–448; 1909 and 1914 eds., pp. 478–485; Gutenberg #53881 = Oct. 1914)
- Intro: "This is a very old and always popular game, which has lately been much improved by the introduction of the variations known as Royal and Spade Cassino, the latter especially being a very lively game. Like Euchre, Cassino is eminently respectable, and is one of the few games of cards that are unhesitatingly admitted to the domestic circle." [S31]
- National-games essay: "Six of these popular games are strikingly typical of the national character … Skat in Germany; Cribbage in England; Piquet in France; Conquian in Mexico; Calabrasella in Italy; and Cassino in America." [S31]
- Markers: "the score may be kept with counters, on a sheet of paper, or on a cribbage board" [S31].
- Stakes: so much a game or so much a point; "if the winner has twice as many points as his adversary, he shall be paid for a double game" [S31].
- Deal: two at a time, "giving two to the table just before helping himself in each round". Positions: "pone" and "eldest hand" [S31].
- Point table: "Cards 3; Spades 1; Ten of diamonds, Big Cassino 2; deuce of spades, Little Cassino 1; Ace of any suit 1; A Sweep of all the cards on the table 1" [S31].
- **Announcements:**
  - "He may place the 2 on the 7, announcing the total value; 'Nine'"; "announcing the total value, 'Ten'".
  - "announcing the build as 'Two Sevens.' This cannot be increased to 8, 9, or 10 under any circumstances"; "announcing, 'Two Nines,'"; partner play "'two Eights,' called" [S31].
  - Combining: "calling attention to the fact that their collective value is 9" [S31].
- Increasing own build: "Some players imagine that a player cannot increase his own build … but there is no reason why a player should be denied a privilege which is freely granted to his adversary." Elsewhere: "It is a common error among Cassino players to hold that a player cannot build on his own build … This is manifestly unfair" [S31].
- "The simple rule to be remembered is that no combination of cards once announced, and left on the table, can be changed, except by the addition of a card from the hand of some player." [S31]
- Trailing: "This is called trailing, because he is simply following along waiting for opportunities. In trailing it is usually the best policy to play the smaller cards, except Aces and Little Cassino" [S31].
- Sweeps "are usually marked by leaving the cards with which they are made face upward … Sweeps made by opposite sides are sometimes turned down to cancel one another" [S31].
- Last cards: "The last trick is usually made by the dealer, who always keeps back a court card if he has one" [S31].
- **Showing** (the end-of-hand count spoken aloud): "each player counts his cards face downward, and announces the number … It should be remembered that the total number of points to be made in each hand, exclusive of sweeps, is eleven, and the total of the claims made must agree with that number." [S31]
- Old scoring: "The old way was to play 11 points up … If one side reached 11 before the adversary reached 6, it was a lurch, and counted as a double game." [S31]
- Twenty-one point game:
  - Order of counting out: "Cards first, then Spades, Big Cassino, Little Cassino, Aces, and Sweeps … the spade Ace goes out first, then clubs, hearts, and diamonds".
  - "It is better to agree to count out … The moment he reaches 21 he should claim the game … If he is mistaken … he loses the game" [S31].
- **Suggestions for Good Play (verbatim):** "The principal thing in Cassino is to remember what has been played especially in the counting and high cards, such as Aces, Eights, Nines, and Tens. In making pairs and combinations, give preference to those containing spades, and if you have to trail, do not play a spade if you can help it. If three Aces have been taken in, play the fourth, if you hold it, at the first opportunity, because it cannot be paired; but if there is another Ace to come, keep yours until you can make a good build with it. As between cards which were on the table and those trailed by an adversary, take in those trailed if you have a choice. Take in the adversary's build in preference to your own, if you can, and build on his build at every opportunity. If Big Cassino is still to come, avoid trailing cards that will make a Ten with those on the table. Go for 'cards' in preference to everything else, and always make combinations that take in as many cards as possible. If you have a Nine, and the cards on the table are 2 2 5 7, take in the 2 2 5, in preference to the 2 7. It is considered bad policy to take in three court cards, as it stops all sweeps when the fourth appears." [S31]
- Variants:
  - Royal Cassino: "The Jack is worth 11, the Queen 12, and the King 13". The 1914 edition adds "The aces are sometimes valued at 14 each" [S31].
  - Spade Cassino: "24 points are made in every hand … Sixty-one points is game, once round the board and into the game hole" (1897 onward) [S23].
  - Draw Cassino: "each player keeps his hand filled to four cards by drawing one from the top of the stock … The stock is left on the table, face down, slightly spread". This is absent in 1897 and present by 1909 [S29].
- Glossary: "Cards … The majority of cards at Cassino." [S31]

## 3. Thematic syntheses

### 3a. Terminology timeline (first attestation among the sources fetched here)
- **Great Cassino / Little Cassino**: 1792 [S1]. Still in Line B texts in the 1870s [S17] and Baxter-Wray 1891 [S21].
- **Big Casino/Cassino**:
  - Townsend 1891 ("Big Casino") [S20];
  - Foster 1897 [S23];
  - USPC 1898 ("Big (or Great) Cassino") [S24];
  - standard by 1929 [S33].
- **Lurch / lurched**: 1792 ("Six Points gained save the Lurch") [S1] through Foster's historical note [S31] and Ostrow's doubling option [S35].
- **Clearing the board → sweep**: "clears the Board" 1792 [S1]; "The Sweep" 1796 [S5]; "sweeping the board" 1891 [S21].
- **Final sweep / last cards**: "final sweep" 1800 [S6]; "Last Cards" glossary 1867 [S15].
- **Building up / build / raise / increase**: 1866 [S14]; "raise the Build" 1894 [S22]; "Increasing Builds" 1897+ [S31].
- **Single build / multiple build**: Ostrow 1945/49 [S35].
- **Call / calling**: 1867 [S15]; "Calling, or Duplicating" 1894 [S22].
- **False build; build from the table**: 1868 [S16].
- **Combine**: 1867 glossary [S15]. The idea is already in 1792 ("equalling any number of Pips") [S1].
- **Lay-out** (the table cards): 1894 [S22].
- **Trailing**: Foster 1897 [S23]; glossed in 1907 [S28] and 1952 [S36].
- **Pone / eldest hand**: Foster [S31]. "Eldest Hand" is in Dick 1867 [S15].
- **Count out / claim the game**: Foster [S31]; "going-out declaration" Ostrow [S35].
- **Showing**: Foster [S31].
- **Cash points**: USPC 1952 [S36].
- **Overs**: Ostrow [S35].
- **Set-back / Rounce Cassino**: Dick's name for the 11-point difference ("European") game, 1867–68 [S15][S16].

### 3b. What players say aloud (a design resource for the video game)
- **Build announcements:**
  - singular number: "Seven", "Nine", "Ten" (1866–67) [S14][S15];
  - "I build seven", "I build nine", "I build four" (1891) [S20];
  - "eight", "nine", "five", "ten" (1907) [S28];
  - "building six" (1952) [S36];
  - "Building eight" (1949) [S35].
- **Call / multiple-build announcements:**
  - plural number: "Fives", "Treys", "Fours", "Nines" (1867–1894) [S15][S22];
  - "I call sixes", "I call fours" (1891) [S20];
  - "Two Sevens", "Two Nines", "two Eights" (Foster) [S31];
  - "fours" (1929) [S33];
  - "Building sevens", "Fives" (1949) [S35];
  - "building fours" (1952) [S36].
- **Announcing is obligatory:** "audibly and distinctly", or the opponent may separate the cards (1867) [S15]; "must be spoken clearly" (1891) [S20]; failing to call lets "adversaries … disperse the cards" (1904) [S25].
- **Timing matters (1894):** saying "nine" on playing an Ace "completed his play", which stops a further duplication from the table [S22].
- **Combining aloud:** "calling attention to the fact that their collective value is 9" [S31].
- **End of hand:** each player "announces the number" of cards he has taken [S31].
- **Claiming the game:** "claim the game" at 21 [S31]; "announce twenty-one at any time" [S20]; "going-out" declaration [S35].
- **Dealer's last-round announcement:** required in 1949/1952 [S35][S36].
- **Disputes:** "every mistake must be challenged immediately" (1796–1867) [S5][S15]. Only the last trick may be looked at [S5][S31].

### 3c. Scoring tools and physical affordances described historically
- Counters, paper, or a cribbage board (Foster) [S31]. A cribbage board is mandatory for Spade Cassino to 61, "once round the board and into the game hole" [S23][S24].
- Sweep markers:
  - the sweep card is left face up in the trick pile [S24][S28][S31][S36];
  - opposing sweeps are turned down to cancel [S22][S31];
  - the face-up card makes sweeps easy to "pick out" at the count [S36].
- Builds are left face up on the table [S36]. The stock for Draw Cassino is left "face down, slightly spread, for convenience in slipping off the top card" [S29].
- Splitting captured pairs to prevent pairs in the next deal: 1792 [S1] through 1875 [S17].

### 3d. Strategy advice: how it changed
- **Constant from 1792 to 1929:**
  - remember the cards played [S1][S6][S31];
  - prefer spades [S1][S5][S31];
  - take the card the opponent just played [S1][S6][S31];
  - don't expose 10s or 2s while the Cassinos are out [S1][S6][S33];
  - trail court cards or small cards, keep aces [S1][S2][S6];
  - try for the last cards [S1][S6][S33].
- **Jones 1796 additions:**
  - card-counting leads ("When the Deuces are out, an eight is an eligible Lead");
  - ace reads (play an ace when the opponent likely has no ace or nine);
  - score-based play (deny the opponent "saving their lurch") [S5][S6].
- **Endgame:** keep a court card for the last cards (1792 [S1]; Foster's dealer [S31]; Seymour 1929 [S33]).
- **Foster's modern priorities [S31]:**
  - "Go for 'cards' in preference to everything else";
  - take the trailed cards and the adversary's builds;
  - "build on his build at every opportunity";
  - make the combination that takes more cards (2-2-5 rather than 2-7 with a nine);
  - avoid trailing cards that make 10 while Big Cassino is out;
  - don't take three court cards because it stops sweeps.
- **Risk management of builds:** progressive building is risky but workable late in the game for a player with a good memory [S20]. It is "generally ruled out as irregular" by 1905 [S27].

- Twenty-one Point Cassino [S20][S22][S31].

- Royal Cassino [S22][S24][S31]. Royal with the 60-card pack [S24]. Royal with the joker as 15 [S27].

### 3f. History and etymology claims made by the manuals
- "Cassino is of Italian origin" (Dick 1866) [S14].
- "of considerable antiquity" (Baxter-Wray) [S21].
- "a very old and always popular game"; typical of America (Foster) [S31].
- Secondary source (Wikipedia, citing Pratesi 1995): there is no evidence of Italian play. The first record is Long 1792. Building and calling are American, 1864/1867. Royal Cassino first appears in Foster 1897 [S38]. **The 1894 Dick and 1898 USPC texts above predate the Royal claim [S22][S24].** The 1864 1st edition of the *American Hoyle* was not fetched, so "building first in 1864" remains [UNVERIFIED]. The earliest building text fetched here is 1866 [S14].

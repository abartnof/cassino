# 13 — Card-table banter in period sources: how players ribbed, teased and talked

Scope: what people said to each other across a card table, in fiction, essays and card manuals from 1714 to the early twentieth century. The aim is new lines for the computer opponent: good-natured ribbing tied to something that is true in the game (a dry spell, a run of luck, a slow move, a close finish, a mistake), grounded in real usage. Notes 04 and 05 covered what cassino players call out (builds, "last", the cards' names). This note covers what they say *about the play and about each other*, which is mostly attested for whist, cribbage, quadrille, speculation, loo, seven-up, poker and bridge rather than for cassino itself.

Method: everything was fetched on 5 October 2026. Each quote was copied from a full text that was downloaded (Project Gutenberg plain text, or an Internet Archive `_djvu.txt` OCR text) and found by search. OCR noise is lightly normalised and marked [OCR]. Nothing is quoted from memory. As a last check, all 218 quoted fragments in the tables were matched by script against the downloaded texts. The 13 that do not match character for character are OCR variants (marked [OCR]), search snippets (FTS) or dictionary citations, and each of those was checked by eye. The lines already in `docs/PHRASES.md` and the sources of notes 04 and 05 were not re-collected; where a source here was already cited there, the overlap is noted.

Conventions:
- Sources are `[13-Sn]`, listed in §1. The period texts are public domain in the United States; the online slang dictionary and the two mid-century bridge books (13-S65, 13-S66) are quoted only briefly, as evidence of dates.
- In the tables, **Said** is the verbatim quote (short, sometimes trimmed with "…"). **Line** is a modern, short, PG adaptation the opponent could say, in plain English, under about eight words. Lines are suggestions, not sourced claims.
- **Kindness rule.** Ribbing about luck, speed, the score or the run of play is in. Anything about intelligence, looks, age, gender, class or nationality is out, even when the original said it (§4 lists what was left out and why). Where an original line carries such a jab, the adaptation drops it and says so.
- Games other than cards (chess, draughts) are flagged; their lines transfer cleanly.
- "UNVERIFIED" marks anything not confirmed in a fetched text.

## 0. Headline findings

1. **Period players did needle the slow player, crow over luck, sigh over bad cards and hold post-mortems.** The manuals prove it by forbidding it. Emily Post (1922): "Don't hold a 'post-mortem' on anybody's delinquencies" [13-S45]; Foster's Hoyle names the post-mortem "'If you hads'" [13-S44]; Cavendish (1879) records that hurrying a slow player "often assumes the form of playful satire", with the stock gibe "Play the one nearest your thumb" [13-S28].
2. **Table talk was expected, even prized, in round games and poker; whist frowned on it.** "Talking is not allowed at Whist; the very word implies 'Hold your Tongue'" (Seymour, quoted by Cavendish) [13-S37]. Poker, by contrast: players may "tell as many cheerful lies about their hands as they please" [13-S44]. The chatty round-game player was a recognised type: Austen's Tom Musgrave had "a lively way of retailing a commonplace, or saying a mere nothing, that had great effect at a card-table" [13-S3]. A talkative cassino opponent sits in the round-game and poker tradition, not the whist one.
3. **The talk was also understood as a tactic.** "Uncle George" (1883) prizes the deaf player, who "is not distracted by talkative players, or those who talk _for a purpose_" [13-S34]; Foster warns of "a fluent speaker who is always trying to 'jolly' the game along" [13-S41]. This is period backing for the design's second purpose: talk that keeps the player from quietly counting.
4. **Luck talk is the richest vein**, in both fiction and manuals: "Never was such luck" (*Pickwick*) [13-S7], "I never had such luck, really" (*Nickleby*) [13-S8], "Luck … is bound to change" (Bret Harte) [13-S15], "I call it a game of science!" (Twain) [13-S14], "Luck is a false friend" (Elwell) [13-S42], "no run of luck can be expected to continue indefinitely" (Curtis) [13-S40].
5. **Good sportsmanship had stock formulas** that make kind closing lines: "Lose without a murmur, win without triumph" (1859) [13-S25]; "a cheerful loser, a quiet winner" (Post) [13-S45]; "perhaps you'll win the next one" (Jacobs) [13-S19].
6. **Many stock sayings are older than they sound; some favourites are not period at all.** "Lucky at cards" is a joke in Swift (1738) [13-S46]; "turn up trumps" is in Grose (1811) [13-S48]; "the game is never lost till won" in Crabbe (1819) [13-S49]; "the cards speak for themselves" in 1831 [13-S50]. "Read 'em and weep" (1918–21) [13-S62][13-S65] and "Deal me in!" (1926) [13-S64] are twentieth-century. "Shuffle up and deal" and "the luck of the draw" have no use before 1950 that could be found (§3.6).

### 3.6 Not found before 1950, or modern only

- **"Shuffle up and deal"**: modern. It is the poker tournament director's opening call; no period use found.
- **"The luck of the draw"**: no use before 1950 in the full-text searches; the earliest Wiktionary citation is 1973. UNVERIFIED as period.
- **"Come to papa"** with cards: only dice uses (above).
- **"Beginner's luck"** in those words: only the idea, in 1898 and 1916 (§3.1).
- **"Loser deals", "winner stays", "let's make it interesting"**: not found as sayings in the Hoyles searched.
- **"Never a borrower…"**: quoted for poker by Keller (1887) [13-S35, p. 64], as Polonius's advice, not as table talk.
- **"The rigour of the game"**: Lamb's, 1821 (§2.1); no earlier use was looked for.

## 4. Left out, and why

These are real period card-table remarks, found in the sources above, that fail the kindness rule. They are listed so that nobody re-finds and re-proposes them.

- **Looks and class.** Estella's first remark at beggar-my-neighbour: "He calls the knaves Jacks, this boy! … And what coarse hands he has! And what thick boots!" (Dickens, *Great Expectations*, ch. VIII, PG #1400). Trollope's "meagre doctor" (§2.2) keeps only "Ah, why so slow".
- **Intelligence.** Miss Crawley's "Poor Briggs can no more play than an owl, she is so stupid" (Thackeray, *Vanity Fair*, PG #599); Saki's "The Sheep" (*The Toys of Peace*, 1919, PG #1477), a whole story about a dim bridge partner; Benson's "gooseberry-idiot" joke (*Miss Mapp*, ch. II). The manuals have more of this, also left out: Mathews's "unqualified by ignorance" [13-S23], Uncle George's "loquacity is indicative of ignorance" [13-S34]. Cavendish's ironic "Duffer's Maxims" [13-S28] and Pembridge's "bumblepuppy" [13-S38] are used here only for their content: the opponent should never call the player a duffer or a bumblepuppy.
- **Age, gender.** "Ah! you are too young and light for this kind of thing" (*Middlemarch*, ch. XVI); "I will stake my last like a woman of spirit" (*Mansfield Park*, ch. XXV: the adaptation keeps "No cold prudence for me!" and drops the gendered phrase); *The Habits of Good Society*'s "women have only two passions, love and avarice" [13-S25].
- **Ethnicity.** Harte's Oakhurst speech in "The Outcasts of Poker Flat" contains a racial slur in the sentence before the one quoted in §2.6; *The Rape of the Lock* compares routed cards to "Afric's sable sons" (III.82). Neither is used.
- **Catty sniping.** Miss Squeers and Miss Price at speculation: "How dull you are tonight!", "Your hair's coming out of curl, dear." (*Nicholas Nickleby*, ch. 9). Funny, but personal.
- **Cheating and gambling ruin.** Quilp's "humorous one of always cheating at cards" (*The Old Curiosity Shop*, ch. 23), the Padre's "Methinks thou art forsworn!" over a revoke (*Miss Mapp*, ch. II), Miles Grendall's loo (*The Way We Live Now*, ch. XXIV), and Nell's grandfather's "Fortune will not bear chiding" (*The Old Curiosity Shop*, ch. 29). Grendall's "one run of luck" line (§2.6) is used without its context. The grandfather's line is apt for bad luck ("Don't scold fortune; she sulks") but belongs to a tragedy of gambling, so it is not proposed.
- **"Muggins".** The cribbage call (§2.9) is a game term, but the word also meant a fool (§3). Use it only as the call, "Muggins!", never as "you muggins".

## 5. Gaps and leads

- **Cassino itself.** Apart from O. Henry's aside, "if casino can be so called" [13-S21], none of the fiction found here shows banter *at cassino*, as opposed to whist, cribbage, speculation, quadrille, loo, seven-up, poker or bridge. Cassino's own table talk is in notes 04 and 05 and is thin. The ribbing transfers because it is about luck, speed and the score, not about rules.
- **Newspapers.** More dated newspaper uses would sharpen §3. Only archive.org's newspaper scans were searched (13-S56, 13-S58, 13-S64). Chronicling America was not tried this time (note 05 found it blocked), and HathiTrust's full-text search sits behind a browser check.
- **Not fetched.** Censor's *Don't* (1883), *Hill's Manual*, Blackbridge's *Complete Poker-Player* (1880), *Poker: How to Play It* (1882), Florence's *Gentleman's Hand-book on Poker* (1892), Pole's *Theory of the Modern Scientific Game of Whist* and "Major A"'s *Short Whist* had no full text that could be reached. Sherwood's *Manners and Social Usages* and the *Bazar Book of Decorum* (1870) were read and have nothing on card talk.
- **More fiction.** Benson's *Queen Lucia* (1920, PG #6840) was searched and has only a passing joke (Georgie's sisters say "I'll play the Guru" whenever they play a knave, ch. 8); Ring Lardner's bridge story "Who Dealt?" (1926) is public domain in the United States but no full text was found; Joseph C. Lincoln's Cape Cod stories (checkers and cribbage among old friends) were not reached. Both are likely sources of gentle ribbing.

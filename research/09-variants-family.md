# 09 — Cassino variants and the fishing-game family: catalog, comparison, genealogy, origin debate

Scope: (1) named Cassino/Casino variants, (2) the wider "fishing" family (Western sum-capture games, single-pile games, Oriental flip-from-stock games), (3) historical ancestors, (4) the Cassino origin debate, (5) mechanics that could become optional modes in the video game. Calls, announcements and scorekeeping aids are flagged wherever they turn up, because the brief asks for them.

Convention: every bullet carries a citation `[Sn]` from the Sources list at the end. Every source was fetched in this session. Quotes are verbatim, with OCR long-s ("ſ"/"f") silently normalised only where noted. Anything not verified is marked **[UNVERIFIED]**.

## 0. Executive summary (key findings)

- **Name of the family.** "Fishing" as a family name was "first called [so] by Michael Dummett" [S70]. McLeod defines it: "Each player in turn plays a card. If it matches a card or cards in the layout, the played card and the matched cards are captured" [S1].
- **Three structural branches** [S1]:
  - **Western layout games:** captures come only from the hand, and "it is generally possible to use a card to capture several cards at once if the ranks of the captured cards add up to that of the played card" (Cassino, Scopa, Escoba, Basra, Pasur, Tablić…).
  - **Single-pile games:** "the table cards are not laid out separately but played in a single pile. Matching the top card enables the whole pile to be captured" (Pishti, Xeri, Mitaines, Ghârat).
  - **Oriental flip-from-stock games:** "after playing a card from your hand … you turn over a further card from the face down stock, and can also capture with that card" (Hanafuda games, Go-Stop, Chinese Ten, Tiu U).
- **Cassino's earliest certain record is London, 1792.** Long's *Short Rules* (1792), a mock-heroic poem with laws (1792, repr. 1793), and *Rules for Cassino* (1795) [S70][S40].
  - It enters Hoyle-type books by 1795–96 [S72][S70].
  - It appears on the stage in 1797 ("I do long for a game of cassino") [S83].
- **The "Italian origin" is a tradition, not a documented fact.** Pratesi searched Florentine *Casino dei Nobili* archives and found no Casino game. He also says Scopa and Scopone "are both documented later than the earliest references to Casino" [S70].
  - McLeod: "there is no direct evidence of it having been played there" [S2].
- **The German line arrives fast and with a twist.** It is printed in Vienna/Prague in 1795 [S70][S40]. By 1810 the German rules give courts 11/12/13, Aces 1/14, Little Casino 2/15 and Great Casino 10/16 [S74].
  - This "Royal" logic survives today in Finnish/Nordic Kasino [S4][S63][S64][S65] and Hungarian Kaszinó [S3].
  - In the English-speaking world the name **Royal Cassino** first appears in print in 1897 [S76].
- **Building and calling are American additions** of the mid-19th century. Building and calling are both in Dick's *American Hoyle* (copyright 1864, 4th ed.) [S75]. Pratesi dates the earliest such print to New York 1866 [S70].
  - The old 11-point "European game" was by then called "Set-back or Bounce Cassino" [S75].
- **Older European fishing games:**
  - Laugh and Lie Down: England, named 1522, described by Willughby c.1665 [S69][S42].
  - Culbas: France, recorded 1658/1665 [S43].
  - Papillon: France, in the *Académie des jeux* tradition from 1730 per Parlett as cited by Wikipedia [S41]; text verified in the 1802 *Académie* [S79].
  - Calabrache: Italy, cited by Pratesi as an "older" game [S70].
  - Snitch'ems: Yorkshire, printed 1773 and 1797 [S27][S73].
  - None of these has sum-building in the Cassino sense. Papillon already has sum-capture [S79].
- **Strongest optional-mode candidates for the game** (section 9):
  - Royal values (courts 11–13, Ace 1/14) [S3].
  - Nordic dual-value hand cards (A=14, ♠2=15, ♦10=16 when played from hand) [S4][S63].
  - Sum-to-15 (Escoba) [S13].
  - Sum-to-11 with Jack-sweeps (Pasur) [S19].
  - Sequence-capture plus "caída" (Cuarenta/Ronda) [S15][S25].
  - Steal-the-pile (Stealing Bundles, African Casino) [S8][S5].
  - Reverse/misère (Krypkasino) [S6].
  - Draw-to-four (Draw Cassino) [S77].
  - Hand declarations (Cirulla/Chorizo) [S11][S14].
  - Single-pile "pişti" [S23].
  - Flip-from-stock (Koi-Koi/Go-Stop) [S52][S36].
  - Sweep-cancelling (Dominican/Finnish/Pasur) [S3][S4][S19].
  - Endgame restrictions near target (Dominican) [S3].

## 1. Capture-mechanic taxonomy (used in all tables below)

| Code | Mechanic | Example games |
|---|---|---|
| P | **Pairing**: equal rank captures equal rank | all; courts only by pairing in Anglo-American Casino [S2] |
| Σ | **Summing**: played card = sum of a set of table cards | Casino [S2], Scopa [S9], Tablić [S16] |
| ΣΣ | **Multi-set summing**: several disjoint sets that each equal the played card are captured at once | Casino [S2], Tablić [S16], Nordic Kasino [S4], Diloti [S21] |
| 1-of | Only ONE set or card per capture; single card beats set | Scopa ("If the played card matches both a single card and the sum of a set of cards, the single card must be captured") [S9]; Kontsina [S20] |
| +N | **Target-sum**: played card + captured cards = fixed N | Escoba N=15 [S13]; Pasur N=11 [S19]; Chinese Ten N=10 [S35]; Snitch'ems N=8 or 10 [S27] |
| B | **Building**: make a pile with an announced value that can only be captured whole | Casino [S2], Seep "houses" [S24], Diloti "declarations" [S21], Zwicker (add or subtract) [S7], Scopa a cascina [S54] |
| Seq | **Sequence capture**: after a match, cards in unbroken ascending run are also taken | Cuarenta [S15], Ronda [S25], Porrazo [S26] |
| J* | **Wild-sweeper**: a special card takes the whole table | Jack in Basra/Pasur/Pishti/Xeri/Žandari [S18][S19][S23][S22][S17]; Ace in Asso pigliatutto/Cirulla [S9][S11]; ♠5 (Danish) [S65]; Jokers (Eléwénjewé amateur) [S34] |
| Pile | **Single-pile**: match top card, take the whole pile | Pishti [S23], Xeri [S22], Mitaines [S30] |
| Steal | **Steal from capture piles** | Stealing Bundles [S8]; African Casino [S5]; Shlla'at [S33]; Ghârat [S32] |
| Flip | **Flip-from-stock**: after playing, turn a stock card that may also capture | Hanafuda games, Go-Stop, Chinese Ten, Tiu U [S1][S35][S36][S38] |

## 2. The Cassino origin debate — all positions found

### Position A — "Italian origin" (traditional)
- The tradition is widespread. McLeod: "Although it is traditionally supposed to have originated in Italy" [S2]. Wikipedia: "often said, without substantiation, to be of Italian origin" (citing Parlett 2008 p. 401) [S40].
- Norwegian Wikipedia (unsourced): "Spillet stammer fra Italia, og er sannsynligvis en avart av det populære italienske kortspillet Scopa" ("The game comes from Italy, and is probably a variant of the popular Italian card game Scopa") [S64].
- Finnish Wikipedia: "pelin uskotaan olevan sukua italialaiselle Scopa-pelille" ("the game is believed to be related to the Italian game Scopa") [S63].
- **Florentine-name version (primary, 1810).** The German *Spielalmanach* footnote: "Die Benennung Casino kömmt von einem Spielhause in Florenz her, worin sich der Adel versammelt" ("The name Casino comes from a gaming house in Florence where the nobility gathers") [S74].
  - Pratesi quotes a near-identical line from Schreiber (1854, p. 267). He adds that Omasta & Ravik (1969) attribute it to "a German book of 1821" [S70].
- **Pratesi's own compromise:** "A possible explanation is that a game, which was commonly played in Italy (probably without written descriptions or known under a different name), was first introduced in England and later modified in America" [S70].
  - He also offers the "single frame" metaphor: "one of the intermediate variants, played at a given place and time, was taken by foreign visitors as the source for Casino … I would be much happier if I could add an exact date, as for instance Florence 1790, but I cannot − yet" [S70].

### Position B — "No evidence for Italy; it is an English game of the 1790s" (sceptical/documentary)
- Pratesi (1995): "the origin of Casino, always said to be of Italian origin, whereas I have not yet been able to trace it here … it is a game widespread from nowhere in Italy" [S70].
  - "this use of the word 'casino' as the name of a specific card game has left no traces in Italy, neither in old handbooks of games, nor in dictionaries" [S70].
  - His archival search of the Florentine *Casino dei Nobili* (founded 1640; enlarged 1761) found only "known ones, as Piquet, Minchiate, Trentuno, and Whist" [S70].
  - Scopa and Scopone "are both documented later than the earliest references to Casino" [S70].
- McLeod: "there is no direct evidence of it having been played there, at least under that name … Casino first appears in the card game literature at the end of the eighteenth century in London, and shortly afterwards in Germany" [S2].
- Wikipedia "Myths of Origin": "detailed research by Franco Pratesi has shown that there is no evidence of it ever being played in Italy" [S40]. Its infobox gives "origin = England" [S40].
- **The London craze is documented.**
  - Pratesi lists three dedicated pamphlets: Long 1792; *A Mock-Heroic Poem … Laws of the Game of Casino* (1792, repr. 1793); *Rules for Cassino* "Humbly Inscribed to Her Grace the Duchess of Marlborough … at Marlborough House, Sion and Brightelmstone" (1795) [S70].
  - He concludes: "The impression derives that a craze for the game occurred in London in the early years of the 1790s" [S70].
  - Literary attestations he cites from the OED: Roberts 1792, Jane Austen 1800, Lady Middleton 1811, Neres 1811 [S70].
  - Reynolds's comedy *Cheap Living* (1797), Mrs Scatter: "I do long for a game of cassino—that is, in the family way—just for a trifle" [S83] (OCR "caffino" normalised).

### Position C — "Gambling-house origin of the name"
- Wikipedia rejects this: "an origin in gambling dens appears unlikely since a casino in the late 18th century was a summer house or country villa; the name was not transferred to gambling establishments until later" (citing Thompson 2015) [S40].
- Pratesi derives the name from the Italian *casino* ("a pleasure-house", "a public room used for social meetings", "finally … a public gambling-house"). He notes Italian *cassino* means "box-cart" and "hardly has any relevance" [S70].
- **Spelling sub-debate.**
  - Pratesi: "The reason for writing Cassino is said to be a printing mistake in one of the early descriptions" [S70]. He prefers "Casino, as it was spelled in the earliest English descriptions (and also in German)" [S70].
  - Wikipedia states the opposite: "The spelling 'Cassino' is used in the earliest rules of 1792" [S40].
  - McLeod: "the earliest sources use the spelling Casino, but a tradition has grown up among later writers to spell it with a double 's'" [S2].
  - The fetched 1795 *Pigott's New Hoyle* uses "Cassino"/"Great Cassino" [S72]. The 1810 German source uses "Casino" [S74].
  - **Conflict noted**: Pratesi gives Long's title as "…Game of Casino" [S70], while Wikipedia gives "…Game of Cassino" [S40]. The original could not be fetched (Google Books blocked).

### Position D — "Papillon / French ancestry"
- Morehead & Mott-Smith (in *Culbertson's Hoyle*, 1950), as quoted by Pratesi: "One of the first references to it in English is found in Hoyle's Games, edited by Charles Jones, London, 1808. But several very similar games are described in earlier French and German manuals, as Papillon and Callabra" [S70].
- An earlier Morehead/Mott-Smith claim, quoted by Pratesi as "puzzling": "Casino descends from French gambling games of the 15th century" (*Hoyle up-to-date*, 1959) [S70].
- Salon des jeux (Académie des jeux oubliés) on Papillon: "Probablement issu d'un jeu plus simple doté du nom moins poétique de Cul-bas, il pourrait bien être à l'origine d'un des trois principaux jeux de cartes joués aujourd'hui en Italie, la Scopa" ("Probably derived from a simpler game with the less poetic name Cul-bas, it may well be at the origin of one of the three main card games played in Italy today, Scopa") [S80].
- AD Poker (French rules site): "Le Papillon est décrit dans l'édition de 1752 des Académies Universelles des Jeux". It says the game resembles Chkouba and is "Scopa in Italy, Tablanette in England, … Casino in the United States" [S81]. This is a weak, popular source.
- Wikipedia (citing Parlett 1990 p. 136): Papillon's "rules first appearing in the 1730 *Académie des Jeux* where it is described as a novelty". It "may have been a development of the older, simpler game of Cul-bas" [S41].
  - The fetched 1802 *Académie universelle des jeux* text opens: "CE jeu qui est presque inconnu à Paris, est cependant très-récréatif" ("this game, which is almost unknown in Paris, is nevertheless very entertaining") [S79].
  - **[UNVERIFIED]**: the 1730 date. The archive.org 1730 scan had no text layer.
- **Assessment (from sources).** Papillon has sum-capture: "un as … un quatre et un cinq; vous pouvez prendre ces trois cartes avec un seul dix" ("an ace, a four and a five: you can take these three cards with a single ten") [S79]. That makes it a fishing game with summing.
  - No source fetched documents a direct line from Papillon to Cassino. The 1790s English texts lack Papillon's shedding objective and its jeton payments [S41][S72].

### Position E — "Chinese origin" (of the family / of Kasino)
- Swedish Wikipedia: "Spelet är av kinesiskt ursprung. I Sverige har kasino spelats sedan början av 1800-talet" ("The game is of Chinese origin. In Sweden kasino has been played since the beginning of the 1800s") [S62]. Its references are popular Swedish books (Glimne 2022; Sundsten & Jäger 2008; Torgny 2003; Nationalencyklopedin 1993); the exact page support was not verified.
- Parlett, more cautiously, says of Laugh and Lie Down: "the earliest known example of a European game of the Fishing family (Cassino, Scopa, etc), which may be of Chinese origin" [S69].
- Hanafuda history: Japanese *Mekuri* (1764–1789 era) is "Mechanically … similar to Chinese fishing games" (citing McLeod & Dummett, *The Playing-Card* 1975) [S51].

### Position F — "Evolved from older European fishing games"
- Pratesi: "with respect to older games, such as Calabrache in Italy or Culbas in France, Casino appears to be a more evolved member. A distinctive character of ancient games of this family is that attention is exclusively or preferentially given to the player having the greatest number of cards" [S70].
- This "majority of cards only" signature fits Snitch'ems (1773/1797) [S27][S73], Laugh and Lie Down [S42] and Culbas [S43].

### Where the debate stands (synthesis, every element cited)
- Earliest dated documents put Cassino in London 1792 [S70][S40]. They put it in Vienna/Prague 1795 [S70][S40].
- Earliest firm Scopone manual: Naples 1855 [S71]. The "1750 Chitarrella" is a later fiction [S56][S71].
- The Italian-origin claim rests on the word *casino* and on a German footnote about Florence [S74][S70]. No Italian game called Casino has been found [S70].

## 4. Catalog A — named Cassino variants (consistent schema)

Schema: **Deck · Players · Deal · Capture · Building · Special cards & points · Sweep · Target · Distinctive · Mode potential**

### 4.1 Classic English Cassino (1790s) — "Set-back / Bounce Cassino"
- **Deck:** 52 [S72][S40]. **Players:** 2; also 3 or 4 (partners "as at whist") [S72][S40].
- **Deal:** 4 each and 4 to the board, first deal only. "It is only on the first deal that any cards are to be turned up on the board" [S72].
- **Capture:** pair, or "make up a number of pips from the cards on the table, equal to the card you lay down" [S72]. Courts are pair-only. No building [S40][S70].
- **Points:** Great Cassino ♦10 = 2, Little Cassino ♠2 = 1, "The cards" (majority) = 3, "The spades" (majority) = 1, each Ace = 1 [S72].
- **Sweep / clearing:** Pigott 1795: clearing the board "you are entitled to add one point" [S72].
  - Pratesi says early counting had "not yet sweep points", and that "sweep" then meant only the final clearing [S70]. Wikipedia notes Long does admit 1 point [S40]. **Sources conflict**; the 1795 primary text supports a point.
- **Scoring/target:** "the lesser number must be subtracted from the greater"; "ELEVEN POINTS CONSTITUTE THE GAME"; lurch = double stake if the adversary wins before you score six [S72].
  - Three-handed: "the two lowest add their points together, and subtract them from the highest" [S72][S40].
  - By 1864 this old form was called "Set-back or Bounce Cassino" [S75]. Pratesi reads "Rounce" [S70].
- **Mode potential:** "Classic 1792 rules" mode with differential scoring and lurch.

### 4.2 American Cassino (Dick/"Trumps", c.1864–1867)
- **Players:** 2 (laws); "Three, four or six persons may play" [S75].
- **Deal:** 4 each "one at a time"; 4 to the board "regularly as he deals, or by one, two, three, or four at a time" [S75].
- **Capture:** "take at once every card of the same denomination upon the board, but likewise all that may combine therewith" [S75].
- **Building ("building up"):** must be announced audibly. "a player, in announcing the denomination of a build, always employs the singular number. Thus: 'Nine' or 'Ten' — not 'Nines' or 'Tens.'" [S75].
- **Calling:** with two or more of a rank, play one onto a matching table card and call it; the plural is used ("Fours", "Fives"). The opponent "cannot combine and take it … with a card of a different denomination" [S75].
- **Penalty:** a build or call without the redeeming card "forfeits two points" [S75].
- **Scoring:** same 11 points plus sweep 1. "Each deal is a game" in the American form [S75][S40].
- **Distinctive:** first printed building and calling, with spoken verbal protocol.
- **Mode potential:** "Calling" toggle (locks a rank against sum-capture).

### 4.3 Modern standard Casino (pagat)
- **Deck/players:** 52; 2–4; 4 as partners [S2].
- **Deal:** 4 + 4 table, dealt in twos. Re-deals: 6 for 2 players, 4 for 3, 3 for 4. Dealer "must announce 'last'" [S2].
- **Capture:** courts pair-only, one at a time. Numerals take equal cards and/or disjoint sets [S2].
- **Building:**
  - Single and multiple builds; you must announce ("building 5") and hold the capturing card [S2].
  - The owner of a build cannot trail [S2].
  - The value of a multiple build is fixed; builds cannot be broken up [S2].
- **Points:** Most cards 3; Most spades 1; each Ace 1; ♦10 "Big Casino or the Good Ten" 2; ♠2 "Little Casino or the Good Two" 1. Total 11 [S2].
- **Sweep:** optional 1 point ("Some players call this a clear"); the sweep card is stored face up [S2].
- **Target:** 21 [S2].
- **Variants** [S2]:
  - Play to 11 (differential).
  - Play to 50.
  - 2 for cards + 1 for last capture.
  - Connecticut/NY: ♦10 = 3, ♠2 = 2, cards 1.
  - Belgian **Wippen**: cards 2, spades 2; sweep = "Wip".
  - Fixed count-out order; claim-out.
  - Building for partner.
- **Slang:** capturing an ace with an ace "is called 'cash' by some players" [S2].

### 4.4 Twenty-one Point Cassino
- First in print in 1880 (Dick). Points are scored as made; a false claim loses [S40].
- Foster 1897: count-out order "Cards first, then Spades, Big Cassino, Little Cassino, Aces, and Sweeps … spade Ace goes out first, then clubs, hearts, and diamonds" [S76].
  - If no one claims and both are out, "the game must be continued to 32 points, and so on, eleven points more each time" [S76].

### 4.5 Royal Cassino (Anglo-American)
- J = 11, Q = 12, K = 13 [S76]. Ace 1 or 14 "at the discretion of the player" per Parlett/pagat [S3][S40].
- Foster 1897: "the three court cards, K Q J, have a pip value, and may be used in combining and building" [S76].
- North-American differences vs Dominican [S3]:
  - Clockwise play.
  - Trailing allowed.
  - Sweeps often unscored, or not cancelled.
  - No near-target restrictions.
  - Count-out priority.
- **USPCC 1900 "monster" Royal:** decks with extra 11- and 12-pip cards make J = 13, Q = 14, K = 15, A = 1/16 [S70].

### 4.6 Spade Cassino
- "every spade counts one point … The spade Jack counts one in addition". This gives 24 points per hand excluding sweeps. Game is 61, "pegged immediately" on a cribbage board [S76][S2].
- **Mode potential:** live-pegging scoreboard UI.

### 4.7 Draw Cassino (Royal Draw Cassino; Swedish *dragkasino*; Finnish *pakkakasino*)
- "no more cards are dealt after the first round, but each player keeps his hand filled to four cards by drawing one from the top of the stock … The stock is left on the table, face down, slightly spread" [S77][S78].
- Called "Royal Draw Cassino" in 1911 [S40]. Swedish *dragkasino* [S62]; Finnish *pakkakasino* ("Neljä käsikorttia täydennetään heti lyömisen jälkeen" = "the four hand cards are topped up immediately after playing") [S63].
- Swazi "crazy casino" is an 8-card draw version [S5].

### 4.8 Diamond Cassino
- Ostrow 1945: "three cards are dealt instead of four and counting occurs by 1 point for cards, for a majority of diamonds, for the 7 of diamonds and for each sweep, whereas 2 points are due for capturing either the four 7s or 6s or Aces" [S70].
- Wikipedia (Parlett 2008 p. 404) adds 40 cards, courts removed, game 11, and calls it a "cross between Cassino and Scopa" [S40].

### 4.9 Give-away Casino (misère)
- "the player gaining the fewest points wins" [S70]. Compare Krypkasino (4.14), Finnish *Laistokasino* [S63], Norwegian *Smyg* [S64].

### 4.10 Partnership (4-player) and 3-player Cassino
- **Partners:** opposite each other; "the same as at whist" [S72]. Pagat allows "building for partner" as a variant [S2].
  - The Dominican rule bans building for partner unless provable, with the announcement "for partner" [S3].
- **3-player:**
  - 1795: each scores separately; the two lowest add and subtract from the highest [S72].
  - German 1810s/modern DE: 7 cards each for 3 players [S58].
  - Pagat: 4 rounds of 4 [S2].

### 4.11 Dominican Republic Casino (Royal)
- **Direction:** counter-clockwise [S3].
- **Ace:** 1/14 [S3].
- **Compulsory capture:** you cannot trail if the played card equals a loose table card [S3].
- **Terminology:** "Captura" / "Cojer", "Forma" (build) [S3]. Missed captures are "dejado" (left behind) or "pisado" (stepped on), and "It is customary for the opponents to point out such cards" [S3].
- **Sweeps ("Virados")** cancel between opponents. The sweep card is placed face-up at right angles under the pile [S3].
- **Endgame restrictions:**
  - At 18 you can win only by most cards; at 19 only by the ♦10 ("diez de casino"); at 20 only by most spades [S3].
  - Soto's variant starts the restrictions at 16 [S3].

### 4.12 Haitian Casino
- Stress on the first syllable, "cásino". Best for four. J/Q/K = 11/12/13, A = 1/14. The scoring appears to be simply most cards [S3].

### 4.13 Nordic Kasino (Sweden, Finland, Norway, Denmark) and Mulle
- **Sweden** [S4][S62]:
  - Royal values, usually no building.
  - Points: most spades 2, most cards 1, Aces 1, ♦10 *storan* 2, ♠2 *lillan* 1, last capture *sistan* 1, sweep *tabbe* 1.
  - Target 16. The dealer warns "sistan" (last) or "båt" (boat).
  - Variant *Överspader*: 1 point per spade over six, target 21 [S4].
- **Finland** [S4][S63]:
  - Dual values. Ace = 1 on the table but 14 from hand; ♠2 (*pieni kasino*) = 2 / 15; ♦10 (*iso kasino*) = 10 / 16.
  - Points total 10 (no last-capture point).
  - Sweep = *mökki* ("hut"); sweeps cancel if all have one.
  - No sweeps in the last deal, or once a player has 10+.
  - Tied cards/spades points roll over "pakkaan" (into the pack) to the next deal [S63].
  - Variants: *Laistokasino* (avoid points; compulsory capture), *Rakennuskasino* (building; 21 target), *pakkakasino* (draw) [S63].
  - Northern-Finland relative *Piitti* [S63].
- **Norway** [S64] (unsourced article):
  - Dual values: ♠2 = 2/15, ♦10 = 10/16, A = 1/14.
  - Building and doubling allowed; points as Swedish.
  - Local names: the game called *tabbe*; the last round *døgga*.
  - Misère: *Smyg* / *krypkasino*.
  - 5-player dealing adjustment.
- **Denmark** [S65] (unsourced article):
  - ♦10 (*Store kasino*) = 10/16, ♠2 (*Lille kasino*) = 2/15, A = 1/14.
  - **♠5 clears the table** ("Spar 5 rydder bordet").
  - Sweep = *svupper*; sweeps usually cancel. Last-trick point *sidsten*; the dealer announces "sidsten".
  - Multiple-build call "mere syv" ("more seven").
  - Alternative rules allow **difference**, even **product and quotient**, in combinations.
  - Target 21.
- **Mulle** (double deck, 104 cards) [S4]:
  - A "mulle" = capturing an identical card (same rank and suit) alone.
  - Sassersson version: 5 each and 5 to the table; mulle = 5 pts.
  - Glimne version: dual values; mulle scores the capture value.

### 4.14 Krypkasino (Swedish reverse Casino)
- 2–6 players, 104 cards [S6].
- **Points:**
  - Scoring cards: ♦10 *storan* 3, ♠2 *lillan* 2, Aces and other spades 1. Total 42 [S6].
  - A forced sweep (*tabbe*) costs +5; the player who set it up gets −5 [S6].
  - Trailing is *krypa* ("creeping") [S6].
- **Distinctive:** zero-sum balancing of the sweep penalty [S6].

### 4.15 Hungarian Kaszinó / "Casino en deux"
- 2 players; **3 cards each**; no building [S3].
- **Multi-card capture from hand:** "A 10 and a 2 on the table can be taken by playing a 7 and a 5 from hand" [S3]. Pratesi calls this "a unique feature" [S70].
- **After a sweep** (*tábla*), the opponent plays twice in a row [S3].
- **Other rules:**
  - First-play sweep scores 2 [S3].
  - Last capture is *csere* [S3].
  - Majority of spades is *pikkje van* [S3].
  - Target 11 [S3].
- **Calls:** a winner stops the game with "**Ausz!**", "**Ausz vagyok!**" or "**Kint vagyok!**" [S3].
- **Scorekeeping:** sweeps are drawn as lines on a slate [S3].

### 4.16 African Casino (Swazi, Sotho, South African; Namibian "Omulongo Womadi")
- Captured cards are kept **face up**; the top cards of opponents' piles can be used in builds [S5].
- **Swazi** [S5]:
  - 52 cards, Royal values.
  - All cards dealt at once. The cutter draws 4 for the layout; re-cut if it is too rich.
  - Capture = "**chow**". A drifting capture-card play = "**drifting**" (also used as partner signalling).
  - Scoring (11 total; no sweep points):
    - Most cards: 2.
    - Most spades: 2.
    - ♠2 ("spy two"): 1.
    - ♦10 ("big ten"): 2.
    - Aces: 1 each.
- **Sotho:** courts lose value [S5].
- **South Africa:** 40 cards (no courts); "at least five spades" = 1 point [S5].

### 4.17 Zwicker / Zwickern (Schleswig-Holstein)
- **Deck:** 52 + 3, 4 or 6 jokers [S7].
- **Values:**
  - Courts double-valued: A 1/11, J 2/12, Q 3/13, K 4/14 [S7].
  - Jokers 15/20/25 (Dithmarschen); 15–30 (Großenwiehe) [S7].
- **Building:** can **add or subtract**; "build for partner" [S7]. In the 6-joker team game you may ask your partner (*Magger*) [S7].
- **Points (Jensen):** jokers 7/6/5, ♦10 3, ♠10 1, ♠2 1 (*Pingel* in the 6-joker version), Aces 1, cards 3, each Zwick 1 [S7].
- **Terms:** sweep = "Zwick" ("equivalent to the English 'tweak'"); layout = "das Bild" [S7].
- **History:**
  - "Zwicker stammt vom alten englischen Spiel Cassino ab" ("Zwicker descends from the old English game Cassino"), as shown by the terms *Lurch* and *Sweep* in the first German sources [S59].
  - First mention 1918; Fallada 1928 "ein ziemlich gerissenes holsteinisches Bauernspiel" ("a rather crafty Holstein farmers' game"); first rules 1930 [S59].
  - "fast nie um Geld gespielt, sondern meist um ein Glas Bier oder einen Grog" (1935) ("almost never played for money, mostly for a glass of beer or a grog") [S59].

### 4.18 Tuxedo (American, Rook cards)
- 40 Rook cards (1–10 in four colours); 2–6 players [S3].
- **Scoring:** most cards 15; "orange" (red + yellow captured together) 10; sweeps 10/5; each Five 5; target 100 [S3].
- **Call:** an **"orange" must be called** or an opponent may call it; a false call costs 10 [S3].

### 4.19 Stealing Bundles ("Steal the Old Man's Bundle/Pack", "Steal Pile") — children's Cassino
- 52 cards; 4 + 4; pairing only (suits ignored) [S8].
- Captures go face-up on your "bundle". "If you play a card that matches the top card of an opponent's bundle, you steal the whole of their bundle". Largest bundle wins [S8].
- Equivalents: Argentina *Casita Robada* (Spanish 40/48), Italy *Rubamazzo* (counter-clockwise) [S8].

### 4.20 Wippen (Belgium/Netherlands) — a graded learning Cassino
- Pagat: Belgian/Flemish scoring with cards 2, spades 2, sweep "Wip" 1 [S2].
- Dutch Wikipedia [S61]:
  - Pairing only for beginners. Add-on rules in order: *optellen* (summing), *bouwen* (building), *bovenbouwen* (raising a build), *dubbel bouwen*.
  - 11 points: cards 2, spades 2, ♦10 2, ♠2 1, Aces 1.
  - "Wip" (sweep) 1; no wip in the last deal.
  - Formula for the number of deals per player count; 1 or 2 packs.
- German Wikipedia: "gut geeignet, um Kinder in die Welt der Stich-Kartenspiele einzuführen und im Zahlenraum bis 10 zu rechnen" ("well suited to introducing children to card games and to arithmetic up to 10"); learners may play open-handed [S60].
- **Mode potential:** a built-in tutorial ladder, from pairing to summing to building to multiple builds.

### 4.21 "Cassino for children" (summary of evidence)
- Pratesi: "Generally one is told that the game is very simple and quite suitable for children, so that they may improve their counting ability" [S70].
- Morehead (*Modern Hoyle*, 1944): "In the average home Casino is known as a children's game, but among gamblers it is known as the finest two-handed game of skill" [S70].
- *Popular Recreator* (1874): "Cassino is a very pleasant and agreeable game, especially for young people" [S70].
- Foster 1897: "Cassino is eminently respectable, and is one of the few games of cards that are unhesitatingly admitted to the domestic circle" [S76].
- Children's relatives:
  - Stealing Bundles [S8].
  - *Asso pigliatutto* ("popular children's game") [S9].
  - Cau Robat ("unusual children's fishing game", Catalonia) [S29].
  - Wippen [S60].
  - Kontsina ("many people in Greece have learnt to play as children") [S20].

### 4.22 Brazilian "Cassino"
- **[UNVERIFIED — no reliable Brazilian rule source fetched]** for a distinct Brazilian Cassino. The Copag rules page (copag.com.br/regras/cassino) returned 403. Web results for Portuguese "cassino jogo de cartas" were gambling spam.
- What is documented is the Brazilian **Escopa / Escopa de 15**, a Scopa/Escoba relative (see 5.4) [S82][S49].

## 5. Catalog B — the wider fishing family (same schema, condensed)

### 5.1 Scopa (Italy)
- **Deck:** 40 Italian (K = 10, Horse/Q = 9, J = 8, 7–A face value) [S9].
- **Players:** 2 (also 3, 4, 6) [S9].
- **Deal:** 3 each + 4 table; re-deal 3s. Throw in if 3+ Kings show [S9].
- **Capture:** 1-of (single card beats set); capture is compulsory if the played card can capture [S9].
- **Points:** scopa 1 each; cards 1; coins 1; settebello 1; primiera 1 (7 = 21, 6 = 18, A = 16, 5 = 15, 4 = 14, 3 = 13, 2 = 12, courts = 10). Target 11 [S9].
- **No scopa on the last card** [S9].
- **Variants** [S9]:
  - Asso pigliatutto (Ace takes all + scopa).
  - Scopa d'Assi.
  - Scopa di Quindici (3 versions).
  - Re Bello.
  - Napola / Napoleone.
  - Scopa con le Scalette.
  - Scopa Bazzica: "Bazzica" (3 cards ≤9) = 3, "Bazzicotto" (trips) = 10, declared after a deal.
  - Sbarazzina; Piva (♣2 = −5).
  - Scopa Corse (36 cards, no 2s, capture optional).
  - Chkobba; Hurrikan.
- **Italian building variant: Scopa a cascina.** "poter giocare una carta sopra a una di quelle in tavola, se si possiede una carta che permette di catturarle entrambe al giro successivo" ("you may play a card on top of a table card if you hold a card that can capture both next turn") [S54].
- **Claim-out:** "chiamarsi fuori" ("calling yourself out") — ending mid-hand by claiming the target; a false claim loses [S9].
- **History:** IT-wiki: "Risulta già diffusa in tutt'Italia nel XVIII secolo, ma non risultano testimonianze precedenti" ("Already widespread throughout Italy in the 18th century, but there is no earlier evidence"; Angiolino & Sidoti 2010) [S54].
  - The "Primiera + Scarabucion, pirates in 15th-century Naples" story rests on a betting-site source (snai.it) [S54][S44]. Treat it as legend.

### 5.2 Scopone / Scopone scientifico
- 4 players in partnership, 40 cards [S10]. Classic deal: 9 each + 4 table, thrown in if 3–4 kings; **Scopone scientifico / a 10 carte**: 10 each, none to the table [S10][S55].
- **Capture:** optional to play a capturing card, but compulsory to take once played [S10].
- **Points:** 4 (cards, coins, settebello, primiera) + scope; target 11 [S10].
- **Variants:** Re bello, Napola, Scopone romano (each coin 1, settebello 2), Scopone alla cieca (blind draw from 10 face-down) [S10][S55].
- **Strategy vocabulary:** "whirlwind" sequences of sweeps; leaving "an anchor"; pairing/unpairing sevens [S10].
- **History:**
  - First manual Naples 1855 (Capecelatro) [S71].
  - De Cesare (1900): "Lo scopone era considerato un giuoco eminentemente scientifico" ("Scopone was considered an eminently scientific game") [S71].
  - The Chitarrella Scopone rules are probably 1930s; the "1750 first edition" was the publisher Pironti's "occasionale frutto della sua fantasia" ("occasional fruit of his imagination") [S56].
  - Pucino claims a Spanish import in the 17th century [S55].
  - Scopone "è rimasto un gioco italiano … l'unica frontiera che ha attraversato è quella con il Canton Ticino" ("has remained an Italian game … the only border it has crossed is into Canton Ticino") [S71].

### 5.3 Cirulla / Cirullone (Liguria) — "ciapachinze"
- **Deck:** 40 French-suited (Genoese pattern) [S11].
- **Capture:** match, sum, **+15**, or **Ace takes all** if no Ace on the table; free choice of capture [S11].
- **Bonuses:**
  - Dealer table total 15 = 1 scopa; 30 = 2 [S11].
  - Hand ≤9 = 3 scope (*barsega*); three-of-a-kind = 10 scope (*barsegon*) [S11].
  - ♥7 wild for bonuses (*poncin* / *matta*) [S11].
  - Declared by "knocking on the table (bussare)" [S11].
- **Points:** la grande (♦K Q J) = 5 (*l'erta*); la piccola (♦A-2-3…) up to 6 (*a bassa*); all diamonds = *cappotto* (instant win); target 51 [S11].
- Character: "lively, noisy and unpredictable" vs Scopone's silence [S11]. IT name *ciapachinze* (acchiappaquindici, "catch-fifteen") [S57].

### 5.4 Escoba / Escoba del 15 / Escova (Pt) / Escopa (Br)
- **Deck:** Spanish 40; Sota 8, Caballo 9, Rey 10 (labelled 10/11/12 — "extremely confusing") [S13].
- **Capture:** played card + table set = **15** [S13].
- **Points:**
  - Dealer table total 15/30 = escoba(s) [S13].
  - Cartas, Oros, "Siete de Velo or Guindis", Setenta [S13].
  - Target 21 [S13].
- **Brazilian Escopa (Copag):** "também conhecida como Escopa de 15 … introduzido no Brasil pelos imigrantes italianos no início do século XX … popularidade nos bairros 'italianos' de São Paulo" ("also known as Escopa de 15 … brought to Brazil by Italian immigrants at the start of the 20th century … popular in the 'Italian' neighbourhoods of São Paulo") [S82].
  - Scoring: all coins 2; majority of coins 1; ♦7 Guindis 1; all four 7s 3; majority 1; majority of 7s 1; opponent <10 cards 2 [S82].
- **Brazilian/Portuguese check-sum rule:** at the end the remaining table cards "devem somar 10, 25, 40 e 55 pontos, não havendo um destes valores significa que houve erro" ("must add up to 10, 25, 40 or 55; any other total means there was an error") [S66][S49].
  - Arithmetic note: the 40-card total of 220 ≡ 10 (mod 15). This is our derivation, consistent with the source rule.
- **Brazilian culture:** "a game of the botequims and bars … played for beer, cigarettes, guaranás" [S49].

### 5.5 Chorizo / Báciga (Uruguay)
- Escoba plus hand declarations: Flor 3, Escalera 3, Tres de Nueve 3, Dos de Miseria 2, **Chorizo (trips) 20**; Báciga (3 cards ≤9) [S14].
- Declare before playing the first card; a false declaration scores for the opponents [S14].

### 5.6 Chkobba / Chkouba (Tunisia, France)
- **Deck/values:** French 40; **Q = 8, J = 9 (reversed)** [S9].
- **Deal quirk:** the cutter's choice of first card [S9].
- **Capture:** compulsory; single beats set [S9].
- **Points** (Arabic names): kārṭa, dīnārī, barmīla (sevens/sixes), sabʿa l-ḥayya (♦7, "the living seven"), chkobba [S9][S68].
- **Target:** 21 [S9].
- **Jargon:** capturing = "ʾakala" ("manger" = to eat) [S68].
- **Origin:** brought to Tunisia by Italian migrants [S9][S68].

### 5.7 Hurrikan (Germany, 1930s)
- 6-card sub-deals; hearts are the bonus suit; "die Premiere"; target 16; double game if the loser has <8 [S9].

### 5.8 Cicera (Brescia) and Scaraboción (Treviso)
- 52-card Bresciane pack (the reason that pack has 52 cards) [S12].
- 12 cards each + 4 table; courts pair-only; capture optional [S12].
- **Points:** cards 2, swords 1, Napula, La Mata (♠2) 1, ♦10 of coins 1, Fante of cups 1 [S12].
- **In-play points:** Scùa (sweep) and **Picada** (capturing the card just played by the left-hand player with an equal card) [S12].

### 5.9 Cuarenta (Ecuador)
- **Deck:** 40 (52 minus 8-9-10) [S15].
- **Deal:** 5 each, no table cards [S15].
- **Capture:** match, sum (numerals only), plus **sequence** [S15].
- **In-play points:**
  - **Caída** (match the card just played) 2 [S15].
  - **Limpia** (clear) 2 [S15].
  - **Ronda** (dealt trips) 4; 4-of-a-kind wins outright [S15].
  - Caída on a ronda card = 10 [S15].
- **Card count:** 20 cards = 6, each card over 20 rounded up to even [S15].
- **Target:** 40 [S15].
- **Restrictions:** none at 30+ for rondas; no limpia at 38 [S15].
- **Scorekeeping:** the unused 8s/9s/10s are the counters; face up = 2 points, face down = 10 (*perro*, "dog") [S15][S48].
- **Customs:** caída "snapped down … with great vigor"; holding the predicted card on the forehead to taunt [S15].
- **Status:** "national card game of Ecuador" [S48]; "Mundial de Cuarenta" since 1968 [S67].

### 5.10 Ronda (Morocco) and Porrazo (SW USA)
- **Ronda** [S25]:
  - Spanish 40; pairing plus sequence; 3-card deals.
  - Announce "**ronda**" (pair) 1 / "**tringa**" (trips) 5; bluffing a false ronda is legal.
  - Caída / este / taper / bouah'd: capturer calls "**one**"; the next equal makes "**five**" (b'khamsa).
  - Clearing (*missa*/*mesa*/*lahsa*); a combined clear + caída is called "**two**", then "**six**", "**eleven**".
  - Target 41.
  - Score kept by chips or by **transferring captured cards** between piles.
- **Porrazo** [S26]:
  - Matching plus sequence plus "porrazo" bonus for taking the previous player's card.
  - Introduced from Mexico; first printed 1900/1901.
  - Played to 61 on a cribbage board.

### 5.11 Tablić / Tablanette / Tabinet (Balkans, Romania)
- **Values:** K = 14, Q = 13, J = 12, A = 1/11 [S16].
- **Deal:** batches of 3; 6-card hands; the cutter sets 4 table cards and shows the bottom card [S16].
- **Capture:** ΣΣ, capture optional [S16].
- **Points:** each A/K/Q/J 1; ♦10 2; other 10s 1; ♣2 1; most cards 3 (= 25) [S16].
- **Sweep:** *tabla* 1; Macedonian "**peeshee**" [S16].
- **Target:** 101; claim-out allowed; tabla restrictions after the 6th hand [S16].
- **Etymology:** *table nette* = "clean table" [S46]. The term first appears in the 1810 German Casino rules as "table nette (Raffel)" for clearing the table [S74][S46].
- **Origin claims:** "from the Orient" (Ulmann 1890) and "from Russia" (Phillips & Westall 1939) [S46].

### 5.12 Žandari (Bosnia)
- Jack ("žandar" from gendarme) clears the table. The cutter keeps any jacks turned into the layout "as a reward for cutting in the right place" [S17].

### 5.13 Basra / Bastra (Egypt, Middle East, Cyprus) and Lebanese Ashush
- **Deck/deal:** 52; 4-card deals; "floor" cards. No jacks or ♦7 allowed in the initial floor [S18].
- **Capture:** pair; numerals sum; Q/K pair-only; **Jack takes all** (not a basra) [S18].
- **♦7:** takes all; it counts as a basra if the floor is all numerals totalling ≤10 [S18].
- **Basra:** 10 points [S18].
- **Points:** majority of cards 30 (ties roll over); jacks and aces 1; ♣2 2; ♦10 3; target 101 [S18].
  - Jordan: basra = twice the capturing card's value [S18].
  - Yemen: double basra (J on J) = 20 [S18].
- **Customs:** experts "play in a constant state of movement … drama and an attempt to distract"; the loser pays for "the shisa and beverages" [S18].
- **Name:** "Greek borrowing from the Arabic"; Pişti "a particular evolution of Basra" (Parlett 1990 p. 138) [S45].

### 5.14 Pâsur / Pasur / Chahâr barg (Iran)
- **Capture:** **sum to 11**; K/Q pair-only; **Jack takes all numerals and jacks** [S19].
- **Sur** (sweep, not by jack, not in the last deal, not at 50+) = 5; Surs **cancel** opponents' Surs [S19].
- **Points:** 7+ clubs (*Haft Khâj*) 7; Aces 1; Jacks 1; ♣2 2; ♦10 3 [S19].
- **Target:** 62. Claim with "**per shodam**" ("I'm full") [S19].
- **Alt names:** Yâzdahtâyi ("eleveny"), "Haft va chahâr, yâzdah" (7 + 4 = 11) [S19].
- **Etymology:** the name entered Persia from Russian *Пасур* in the 19th century [S47].

### 5.15 Greek: Kontsina, Xeri, Diloti
- **Kontsina:** 4-card deals; 1-of capture [S20].
  - Points: cards 2, clubs 1, "the good 2" (♣2) 1, "the good 10" (♦10) 1. These translate "«το 2 το καλό» and «το 10 το καλό»" [S20].
- **Xeri (Ξερή, "dry"):** single pile; jack takes the pile [S22].
  - Xeri (taking a lone card) = 10; jack-on-jack = 20 [S22].
  - Points: A/K/Q/J/10 1; good 10 +1; good 2 1; cards 3 [S22].
- **Diloti (Δηλωτή, "declared"):** Kontsina plus Xeri plus **declarations** [S21].
  - Plain declaration (raisable only by the opponent) and group/"family" declaration. Players say "eight" or "two 9's"/"group of 9's"/"family of 9's" [S21].
  - Courts pair-only; you cannot lay a court if its twin is on the table [S21].
  - Points: cards 4, Aces 1, good 10 2, good 2 1, xeri 10. Target 61 [S21].

### 5.16 Pişti / Pişpirik (Turkey)
- Single pile; jack takes the pile [S23].
- **Pişti:** ("cooked") = 10; jack-on-jack 20 [S23].
- **Points:** J 1, A 1, ♣2 2, ♦10 3, cards 3. Target 151 [S23].
- **Bluff variant:** play face-down and claim a pişti; if challenged and true it scores double [S23].
- **Scorekeeping:** tally marks for tens forming a "gate" for fifty [S23].

### 5.17 Mitaines / Mitts (French Canada) and Hockey (Canada)
- **Mitaines:** single pile; scores for sets played to the pile: *mitaine* (pair), *gant* (3), *chausson* (4) [S30].
- **Hockey:** invented 1961–62 in Eddystone, Manitoba [S31].
  - Two "periods" plus overtime; "breakaway"/"clearing the ice"; "goals" [S31].

### 5.18 Seep / Sip / Sweep (N. India, Punjab, Pakistan)
- 4-player partnership; all cards dealt after an opening **bid for a house** (9–13) [S24].
- **Houses** (*ghar*): ordinary, or cemented (*pukka*). Only one house per value [S24].
- **Points:** spades = capture value; other Aces 1; ♦10 6 (total 100) [S24].
- **Sweep:** 50 (25 on the first play, 0 on the last) [S24].
- **Game:** a 100-point lead = *baazi* [S24].
- **Older 30-point Punjab version** [S24].

### 5.19 Shlla'at (Sudan), Ghârat (Iran), Eléwénjewé (Nigeria/Yoruba)
- **Shlla'at** ("uprooted") [S33]:
  - 54 cards; jokers 50.
  - Chain captures: after each capture, draw and continue.
  - Can capture from other players' piles.
- **Ghârat** ("loot") [S32]:
  - Single pile.
  - Can also capture the top cards of opponents' capture piles.
- **Eléwénjewé** [S34]:
  - Yoruba; "dates from the slave trade era".
  - Capture = "scoop".
  - Amateur jokers clear the table; "professional" two-player play bans them.

### 5.20 Snitch'ems / Snitcham (Yorkshire, 1773/1797)
- 3-card deals; capture by pairs/prials or by **making eights and tens**; compulsory capture; majority of cards wins [S27].
- Primary text (Dec 1797): "endeavour to make with his own card, and those on the table, either eighths, tens, pairs, or pair royals" [S73].
- Also: "it is always the judgment to rid the table of the lowest cards" [S73].

### 5.21 Laugh and Lie Down (England, 16th–17th c.)
- **Players/deal:** 5 players; 8 each, 12 "scattered randomly" face up [S42][S69].
- **Capture:** win cards in pairs; prials and "mournival" [S42][S69].
- **Lie down:** when unable to capture you "lay down" your hand "whereupon the other players are supposed to laugh at you" [S69].
- **Settlement:** in chips, relative to eight cards [S42].
- **Willughby:** "There is no other Game at cards that is any thing a kin to this" [S69].

### 5.22 Culbas / Cul-bas (France, 1658) and Papillon (France, 1725/1730)
- **Culbas** [S43]:
  - Pairing only; 5 each, 8 table.
  - Unable to play → "culbas" (throw your hand down).
  - The first to shed wins the pool.
- **Papillon** [S41][S79]:
  - 3–4 players; 3 each, 7 table (3 players) or 4 (4 players).
  - Sum-capture of numerals; K/Q/J and 10s pair-only per the *Académie* [S79].
  - Bonus feats: *Hanneton* (capturing three of a rank with the fourth in hand), *Sauterelle* (sweep), *Petit Papillon* [S41].
  - Winner "Papillon" = first to shed after the talon runs out [S41].
  - Jetons/fiches pool [S41][S79].

### 5.23 Callabra
- Wikipedia (citing Parlett 2008 pp. 418–419): "fast and simple forerunner of Cassino". 3 each, 5 to the table; J/Q/K = 11/12/13; take with one card or **two hand cards summing**; ends when someone clears the table [S40].
- Morehead & Mott-Smith (1950) pair "Papillon and Callabra" as earlier French/German games [S70].
- Pratesi separately cites the Italian **Calabrache** as an older fishing game [S70].
- **[UNVERIFIED]**: Callabra's primary source, and whether "Callabra" is a garbling of Calabrache. Parlett's books were not accessible (archive.org 401/403).

### 5.24 Cau Robat (Catalonia)
- Spanish 40; children's game; "Cau calls" and sequence captures; payment in cards [S29].

### 5.25 Oriental "flip" games (Hanafuda/Hwatu/Chinese cards and dominoes)
- **Hachi-Hachi (Japan)** [S37]:
  - 48 hanafuda cards; 3 active players (up to 7 at the table).
  - Total value 264, par 88.
  - **Yaku** stop play; **teyaku** declared on the deal.
- **Koi-Koi (Japan)** [S52]:
  - 2 players; *oya* (dealer) deals 8 each + 8 table.
  - Match by month, then flip.
  - On forming a **yaku**, choose stop or continue ("koi-koi" = "come on"); 12 hands = 12 months.
  - *Oya-ken* (dealer's privilege).
  - Origin: per Ebashi (2014), Koi-Koi is "partially derived from Go-Stop" [S53].
- **Go-Stop / Godori / Matgo (Korea)** [S36][S53]:
  - 2–3 active players; call "Go" (continue, multiplier) or "Stop".
  - Special events: *ppeok/ppuk*, *jjok/chok*, *ttadak*, *sseul* (sweep), shaking (*heundeulgi*), *poktan* (bomb), *nagari* (null game).
  - "invented in the 1960s … very popular in South Korea in the 1970s" [S53].
- **Chinese Ten (Jiăn Hóng Diăn, 揀紅點)** [S35]:
  - 52 cards; numerals capture by **summing to 10** (one card only).
  - Tens/courts pair-only; flip after each play.
  - Relatives: Thai "Red Frog Black Frog", Indonesian "Main Merah".
- **Tiu U (Chinese dominoes, "fishing")** [S38]:
  - Two double-six Chinese domino sets; match pip totals; draw after each turn.
  - Recorded by Culin (1895).
- **Correction to the task prompt:** **Kap Tai Shap** (K'ap Tái Shap, "collecting tens") is **not a fishing game**.
  - Pagat classes it among "rummy games" with Chinese dominoes, separately from "fishing games (such as Tiu U)" [S39].
  - Celko's rules: "It is like a Rummy game played with Chinese dominoes" [S87].
  - Tiến lên is a climbing game and is excluded, as the prompt anticipated.

## 8. Scorekeeping aids and physical conventions by variant (feeds brief item b)

- **Face-up "sweep card" in the capture pile.** This is near-universal:
  - Casino [S2].
  - Scopa: "placed face up and sideways" [S9].
  - Dominican: "at right angles … partly visible" [S3].
  - Swedish tabbe: "rotated so that the card remains visible" [S4].
  - Zwicker: use a non-scoring card [S7].
  - Basra and Xeri: perpendicular [S18][S22].
- **Cribbage board:**
  - Spade Cassino to 61 [S76].
  - Foster: score "with counters, on a sheet of paper, or on a cribbage board" [S76].
  - Porrazo to 61 [S26].
- **Spare cards as counters:** Cuarenta's 8/9/10s; face down = *perro* (10) [S15][S48].
- **Card transfer between capture piles** as score (Ronda) [S25].
- **Tally gates:** Pişti tens with a closed "gate" for fifty [S23].
- **Slate lines:** Hungarian Kaszinó sweeps [S3]. Tablić tablas as tally marks on the score sheet [S16].
- **Chips/jetons/fiches pools:** Papillon, Culbas, Laugh and Lie Down [S41][S43][S42].
- **Joker values written on the cards:** Zwicker (6-joker packs, sold 1950s–c.2020) [S7][S59].
- **Exposed bottom card or cut card:** Tablić [S16], Xeri variant [S22], Pişti [S23], Hungarian (the cutter sees it) [S3].
- **End-of-hand checksum:** Escova/Escopa table total must be 10/25/40/55, otherwise an audit [S66][S49].

## 10. Conflicts and discrepancies noticed

- **Earliest spelling** (Casino vs Cassino in Long 1792): Pratesi gives "Casino" [S70]; Wikipedia gives "Cassino" [S40]. The primary source was not fetched.
- **Sweep points in the 1790s:** Pratesi says none [S70]. Pigott 1795 gives "add one point" [S72]. Wikipedia says Long gives 1 [S40].
- **Date of American building/calling:** Wikipedia says 1864 building and 1867 calling [S40]. The archive.org copy (copyright 1864, "Fourth Edition") already has both [S75]. Pratesi gives 1866 [S70], and Parlett (quoted by Pratesi) 1868 [S70]. The printing date of the fetched copy is uncertain.
- **Laugh and Lie Down first record:** 1522 (Skelton, OED, via Parlett) [S69] vs "1515" [S43].
- **Papillon's first date:** 1725 [S43]; 1730 [S41]; "1752 edition" [S81].
- **Koi-Koi ↔ Go-Stop direction:** Go-Stop derived from Hana-awase/Hachihachi, while Koi-Koi is "partially derived from Go-Stop" (Ebashi 2014) [S53]. This is a counter-intuitive claim; flag it for verification.
- **Origin of Cassino:** Italy [S64][S63] vs England [S40][S70] vs China [S62]. See section 2.
